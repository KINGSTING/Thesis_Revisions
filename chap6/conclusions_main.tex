\chapter{Conclusions}

This study set out to address the persistent gap between policy formulation and on-the-ground reality in the implementation of the Ecological Solid Waste Management Act (R.A. 9003) within the Municipality of Bacolod, Lanao del Norte. By departing from traditional static policy analysis and adopting a computational approach, this research successfully developed a coupled Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) framework. This novel system went beyond simple waste generation metrics to simulate the complex behavioral dynamics of 3,923 household agents across seven heterogeneous barangays. This granular simulation allowed a governing AI agent to autonomously explore the policy space and discover optimal resource allocation strategies under strict budgetary constraints.

The project concluded that the primary driver of non-compliance in the municipality was not a lack of awareness, but the high ``Cost of Effort'' associated with segregation. The DRL agent's independent discovery of the ``Sequential Saturation'' strategy—which prioritized the reduction of logistical friction and targeted enforcement over broad, diluted educational campaigns—provided a data-driven refutation of the current ``Status Quo'' approach. It demonstrated that equity in funding did not equate to efficacy in performance, offering a replicable roadmap for adaptive local governance.

\section{Achieved Objectives}

The study successfully met its research objectives by constructing a cohesive pipeline from data synthesis to policy optimization. The foundation of this success lay in the rigorous synthesis of demographic data from the Philippine Statistics Authority, financial constraints from the Municipal Environment and Natural Resources Office (MENRO), and behavioral parameters from regional literature. This data fusion created a high-fidelity simulation environment that accurately reflected the socio-economic heterogeneity of Bacolod’s coastal and urban barangays. Building upon this, the study constructed a multi-level Agent-Based Model where household decisions were governed by a dynamic Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) utility function. This model successfully captured the non-linear emergence of ``Cultural Inertia'' and validated the critical influence of social norms, demonstrating how individual household decisions aggregated into divergent barangay-level compliance rates.

The core technical objective was achieved through the integration of a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent into this ABM environment. The agent successfully learned to navigate the continuous action space of budget allocation, balancing three distinct policy levers—Information, Education, and Communication (IEC), Incentives, and Enforcement—to maximize compliance without exceeding the \textpeso 1.5 million annual budget cap. Through extensive simulation and Global Sensitivity Analysis (GSA), the study validated the efficacy of the AI-derived ``Sequential Saturation'' strategy. The analysis confirmed that this adaptive approach, which targeted the ``Convenience Barrier'' ($S_1 \approx 0.83$) by concentrating resources to break inertia in specific locales, significantly outperformed static ``Pure Penalty'' or ``Pure Incentive'' regimes. This offered a statistically robust solution to the municipality's compliance stagnation, proving that targeted intensity yielded higher returns than diluted equity.

\section{Critique and Limitations}

While the proposed framework offered significant advancements in policy optimization, it was subject to specific limitations inherent to its design and scope. The primary critique of the model was its reliance on synthesized behavioral weights derived from regional proxy data rather than primary household surveys specific to Bacolod. While sensitivity analysis confirmed the model's robustness to parameter variance, the absence of direct empirical behavioral calibration meant the behavioral ``tipping points'' identified were probabilistic estimates rather than exact predictions. Furthermore, the simulation was operationally limited to the seven barangays currently covered by the municipal waste collection system. The nine inland barangays, which faced distinct logistical challenges such as lack of road access, were excluded. Consequently, the optimal policies identified might not have been directly transferable to these geographically isolated areas where the ``Cost of Effort'' was likely significantly higher due to infrastructural deficits.

Additionally, the model operated under an assumption of institutional integrity. It assumed that enforcement agents (Eco-warriors) operated with perfect compliance to the policy, effectively ignoring the real-world friction of corruption, ``tipping,'' or the social hesitancy of barangay officials to penalize their own constituents. By excluding these informal transactions, the model likely represented an ``optimistic'' upper bound of enforcement efficacy. Finally, the study abstracted the physical logistics of waste collection—such as truck routing, fuel costs, and bin capacity—into a generalized ``Infrastructure Score.'' It did not optimize the physical movement of waste, meaning the ``convenience'' improvements were modeled as financial investments rather than specific engineering solutions.

\section{Future Work}

To further bridge the gap between simulation and implementation, future research should expand the model's complexity to encompass the ``hidden'' dynamics of local governance. Future iterations should introduce ``Dishonest Enforcers'' or ``Bribable Agents'' into the simulation. By using Game Theory to model the interactions between non-compliant households and corruptible officials, researchers could test the resilience of the AI's policies against systemic corruption and identify mechanisms to disincentivize informal tipping. Furthermore, the model should be expanded to include the nine inland barangays, integrating a ``Logistics Agent'' responsible for optimizing the physical collection routes. This would transform the study into a dual-optimization problem: simultaneously optimizing the \textit{behavioral policy} for residents and the \textit{operational logistics} for the LGU trucks.

The most significant avenue for future work would be a real-world validation via a longitudinal pilot study. The ``Sequential Saturation'' strategy should be piloted in a single test barangay, such as Liangan East, for a period of six months. Comparing the real-world compliance data from this pilot against the simulation's predictions would provide the ultimate validation of the ABM-DRL framework, moving the system from a theoretical tool to an operational asset.

\section{Final Remarks}

This thesis demonstrated that the challenge of Solid Waste Management in the Philippines was not merely a logistical engineering problem, but a complex behavioral one. The findings revealed that well-intentioned policies often failed not because of a lack of funding, but because of a misalignment between the policy instruments and the behavioral drivers of the population.

The Deep Reinforcement Learning agent did not simply find a ``better number''; it discovered a fundamental truth about the local context: that residents were rational actors constrained by the ``Convenience Barrier.'' By proving that reducing the friction of compliance was far more effective than punishing non-compliance or preaching awareness, this study offered the Municipality of Bacolod a scientifically grounded path forward. It signified a shift from ``hit-and-miss'' governance to evidence-based policy design, ensuring that every peso of the municipal budget was invested where it would yield the highest return for the environment and the community.
