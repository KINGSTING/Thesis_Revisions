\chapter{Proposed Smart Governance Framework}

This chapter synthesizes the computational findings into an actionable, evidence-based governance strategy for the Municipality of Bacolod. By bridging the gap between Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) outputs and socioeconomic realities, the evaluation uncovers the specific mechanisms that dictate solid waste management (SWM) success in the Philippine local government context \citep{Jimenez2025}.

\section{Setting the Governance Context}

\subsection{Policy Implementation and Governance Realities}
\label{sec:governance_reality}

Before presenting the proposed Data‑Driven Solid Waste Management (DD‑SWM) Framework, it is essential to ground the computational findings in the actual political and administrative context of the Municipality of Bacolod. The simulation results, while mathematically rigorous, do not operate in a vacuum. Real‑world policy implementation is shaped by street‑level bureaucrats, front‑line discretion, political patronage, and the often‑fragile role of civil society. This subsection translates the abstract policy levers of the Agent‑Based Model into these governance realities and explains why the HuDRL‑discovered “Sequential Saturation” strategy must navigate, not ignore, these constraints.

\paragraph{Street‑Level Bureaucracy and Front‑Line Discretion.}
The Environmental Enforcement Agents in the simulation—whether Barangay Tanods or Municipal Strike Force personnel—correspond directly to street‑level bureaucrats in the public administration literature \citep{Lipsky1980}. These front‑line workers exercise considerable discretion because the formal ordinance (Municipal Ordinance No. 2018‑05) cannot prescribe every inspection, every fine, or every warning. In our key informant interviews, Barangay officials repeatedly stated that they “just remind” rather than issue tickets, and that enforcement is often selective due to manpower and political sensitivity. The simulation’s distinction between low‑capacity, high‑discretion Tanods (90\% stochastic distraction) and high‑capacity, low‑discretion Strike Forces reflects this reality. However, the model assumes honest execution; in practice, discretion can lead to uneven enforcement, favouritism, or even bribery (as hinted in the Liangan East interview). Therefore, the proposed DD‑SWM Framework must be accompanied by clear standard operating procedures and oversight mechanisms that limit harmful discretion while preserving the flexibility needed to adapt to local conditions.

\paragraph{Political Dynamics and Patronage.}
The simulation includes a political capital variable that penalises excessive fines and triggers collapse when approval drops below 10\%. This is a deliberate abstraction of the electoral and patronage dynamics that pervade Philippine local governance. Elected officials are acutely sensitive to voter backlash; imposing fines on a large fraction of households is politically suicidal, as the MENRO Head confirmed: “If we were to be extremely strict, all households would be penalised, which isn’t feasible.” Beyond approval ratings, patronage politics affect resource allocation. Barangay chairpersons may redirect SWM funds to other priorities (e.g., infrastructure or livelihood) to reward political supporters, a behaviour not modelled in our static baseline budgets. The HuDRL agent’s “Sequential Saturation” strategy—concentrating municipal funds on one barangay at a time—could be politically challenging because it appears unfair to those not selected first. Yet the simulation shows that equal distribution leads to universal failure. Overcoming this requires the executive branch to use the AI’s recommendations as a “mathematical shield” to justify temporally unequal but systemically efficient allocation, a point elaborated in Pillar I.

\paragraph{Role of Civil Society Organizations and Advocacy.}
The simulation only models government agents (Mayor, Barangay, Enforcement) and households. It does not explicitly include civil society organisations (CSOs) such as environmental NGOs, women’s groups, or youth organisations (Sangguniang Kabataan). In reality, CSOs play a crucial role in advocacy, monitoring, and behaviour change. For example, the Eco‑brick exchange programmes described in several barangay interviews were often initiated by SK or local volunteers, not by the LGU. These grassroots efforts can sustain compliance when government funding is exhausted—effectively acting as an external reinforcement of social norms. The simulation’s “Social Norm Shield” approximates this effect but does not capture the organisational effort required to build and maintain such movements. The DD‑SWM Framework should therefore include a mechanism for CSO engagement: co‑designing IEC campaigns, participating in monitoring (e.g., “Citizen’s Watch”), and providing feedback to the LGU. Without this, even the optimal algorithmic policy may fail due to low community ownership.

\paragraph{Connecting Simulated Policies to Governance Constraints.}
The comparative results (Section~\ref{sec:comparative_policies}) directly mirror governance failures observed in practice. The Status Quo (equal distribution of IEC) fails because street‑level bureaucrats lack the resources to enforce the “No Segregation, No Collection” rule—a classic implementation deficit. Pure Enforcement collapses because political patrons withdraw support when their constituents are heavily fined—a patronage backlash. Pure Incentives fails because front‑line barangay treasurers cannot sustain reward payouts, leading to perceived unfairness and loss of trust—a breakdown in fiscal administration. Only the HuDRL strategy, which concentrates resources to trigger a social norm lock‑in and then rotates to the next target, acknowledges that governance resources are finite and that behavioural change requires a critical mass of visible, sustained intervention. This aligns with the theory of “policy sequencing” in public administration: policies that would be ineffective if applied everywhere simultaneously can succeed when deployed in a phased, targeted manner, provided that political and bureaucratic actors are willing to accept short‑term inequity for long‑term sustainability.

In summary, the technical optimisations of the HuDRL agent are not a substitute for sound governance; they are a decision‑support tool that must be implemented through capable street‑level bureaucrats, managed with political acumen, and complemented by active civil society. The following four pillars of the DD‑SWM Framework are therefore designed to be governance‑aware, not merely algorithmically optimal.

\subsection{Problem Analysis and Core Issues}
\label{subsec:problem_analysis}

Before presenting the four pillars of the Data‑Driven Solid Waste Management (DD‑SWM) Framework, it is essential to articulate precisely which governance failures the simulation is designed to address and how its insights translate into actionable policy levers – while also acknowledging the structural and political constraints that bound any real‑world intervention.

\paragraph{The Core Problem: A Tripartite Failure.}
The simulation environment and the subsequent comparative analysis (Section~\ref{sec:comparative_policies}) target three interconnected failures that characterise solid waste management in the Municipality of Bacolod and similar resource‑constrained LGUs.

\emph{First, chronically low household compliance.} The empirical baseline (Section~\ref{subsec:empirical_init}) confirms that source segregation hovers around \(12.5\%\), far below the level required to make Material Recovery Facilities and collection systems operationally viable. This is not primarily a knowledge deficit – residents possess positive environmental attitudes – but a behavioural gap driven by high physical “Cost of Effort” (Sobol first‑order index \(0.79\)). The model therefore isolates the failure not in awareness but in the daily inconvenience of washing, sorting and storing waste.

\emph{Second, severe budget constraints at both municipal and barangay levels.} The annual municipal augmentation fund is fixed at P1.5 million, while seven barangays operate on baseline SWM budgets ranging from P15,000 (Babalaya) to P200,000 (Poblacion). The “dual‑budget” architecture of the ABM reflects the real fragmentation of fiscal authority under the Local Government Code. The core optimisation problem is how to allocate a small, top‑up budget across heterogeneous jurisdictions, each with its own local spending patterns and behavioural inertia.

\emph{Third, enforcement gaps that render the “No Segregation, No Collection” rule largely symbolic.} In the simulation, Barangay Tanods suffer a \(90\%\) stochastic distraction rate, and municipal Strike Forces are expensive and temporary. This mirrors the MENRO’s admission that manpower is “kulang” and that strict municipality‑wide enforcement would penalise “all households”. The model operationalises enforcement as a spatially constrained, probabilistic detection process, revealing that deterrence only becomes effective when the density of enforcers reaches a critical threshold – a threshold that current budgets rarely meet.

\paragraph{Policy Levers Identified by the Model.}
The HuDRL agent’s search over the 21‑dimensional action space identifies three primary levers that can influence compliance, and the Global Sensitivity Analysis quantifies their relative effectiveness.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Resource allocation (targeting):} The single most powerful lever is the \emph{spatial concentration} of funds. Equal distribution (the Status Quo) dilutes the per‑capita intervention intensity to the point where no barangay reaches the \(70\%\) tipping point. The agent’s discovered “Sequential Saturation” strategy – concentrating up to \(90\%\) of the quarterly budget on one barangay at a time – demonstrates that the same total budget can produce radically different outcomes purely through the timing and targeting of allocations. This speaks directly to the LGU’s ability to re‑write appropriation ordinances to allow phased, unequal spending.
    \item \textbf{Enforcement intensity and “pulsed” monitoring:} The Pure Enforcement scenario shows that a short, massive surge of inspectors can forcibly raise compliance to over \(90\%\), but it also triggers political collapse within two quarters. The model therefore suggests that enforcement is a powerful but brittle lever. The optimal use is \emph{pulsed} – heavy during the initial saturation phase of a barangay, then quickly scaled back once the social norm shield (Section~\ref{subsec:habit_shield}) locks in behaviour. This insight is actionable: the LGU can design “strike force” deployments that are explicitly temporary and rotated.
    \item \textbf{Incentives as a catalytic, not sustaining, lever:} Pure Incentives fail because the fixed budget is exhausted by early adopters, and the remaining households perceive unfairness. However, the performance jackpot in the reward function (Section~\ref{subsec:reward_shaping}) shows that incentives are highly effective \emph{when paired with concentrated enforcement and education} during the critical period before a barangay reaches \(70\%\) compliance. In the HuDRL strategy, incentives are used heavily in the first two quarters of a target barangay, then reduced to near zero. Thus, the model reframes incentives as a start‑up catalyst rather than a permanent subsidy.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Structural and Political Constraints on Policy Levers.}
The simulation is deliberately abstract, but its parameters and failure modes directly correspond to real‑world constraints that limit what even an optimal algorithmic policy can achieve.

\emph{First, the political cost of unequal allocation.} The Sequential Saturation strategy is mathematically optimal, but it requires the executive to openly favour one barangay over others for several quarters. In a patronage‑driven system, mayors and councillors fear accusations of bias. The MENRO Head’s statement that “all households would be penalised if we strictly enforced” reflects this political reality. The model cannot solve this; it can only provide a “mathematical shield” – a data‑driven justification that unequal allocation is a temporary means to universal compliance. Whether elected officials are willing to use that shield is a political, not computational, question.

\emph{Second, the irreducibility of physical friction without infrastructure investment.} The Sobol sensitivity analysis shows that the Cost of Effort dominates all other behavioural parameters. The model’s lever for reducing effort is purely financial (incentives and enforcement do not directly lower physical hassle). In reality, reducing effort requires capital expenditure: repairing collection vehicles, distributing free bins, constructing accessible MRFs. These are not part of the quarterly augmentation budget. Thus, the model highlights a hard constraint: even the optimal allocation of the P1.5 million fund cannot fully compensate for broken collection vehicles or the absence of basic infrastructure. Policy must therefore complement algorithmic optimisation with physical investment – a point elaborated in Pillar II.

\emph{Third, the fragility of the social norm shield in the absence of civil society.} The simulation’s “norm lock‑in” at \(70\%\) compliance is a heuristic that assumes once peer pressure reaches a threshold, it sustains behaviour without ongoing government funding. In practice, social norms require maintenance through community organisations, regular reminders, and visible role models. The model does not include civil society actors (NGOs, SK, religious groups) that can perform this function. Therefore, the optimal policy discovered by HuDRL may fail in reality if the LGU does not actively cultivate and support grassroots champions. This is a structural constraint that the framework acknowledges but does not solve; it must be addressed through separate governance mechanisms.

\paragraph{Synthesis: What the Model Can and Cannot Say.}
The simulation provides rigorous comparative evidence that, under its assumptions, Sequential Saturation outperforms static heuristics. It identifies the three levers – targeted allocation, pulsed enforcement, and catalytic incentives – as the most promising for the LGU to test in a small‑scale pilot. However, the model cannot predict whether the political system will tolerate unequal allocation, whether the municipal engineer can repair the MultiCab in Liangan East, or whether the Barangay Health Workers will voluntarily sustain IEC after funding is reduced. These are not failures of the model; they are the boundaries within which computational optimisation must operate. The four pillars that follow are designed to work \emph{within} these boundaries, not to pretend they do not exist.

\subsection{Empirical Evidence and Interdisciplinary Foundations}
\label{subsec:empirical_foundations}

The Data‑Driven Solid Waste Management (DD‑SWM) Framework, and particularly its first pillar of algorithmic resource targeting (Sequential Saturation), raises a legitimate concern: will citizens accept a policy that visibly favours one barangay over others? Professor John’s observation that Pillar I may fail due to citizen reaction, perceived bias, and political resistance is precisely the kind of real‑world constraint that computational models must confront. This subsection addresses that concern by (a) reviewing empirical evidence on public perceptions of algorithmic resource allocation, (b) distinguishing what the simulation can claim from what it cannot, and (c) situating the work at the intersection of computer science, public policy, and governance.

\paragraph{Empirical Evidence on Algorithmic Resource Allocation and Public Perceptions.}
The concern that concentrated, sequential resource allocation may be perceived as unfair is empirically well‑grounded. Research on algorithmic decision‑making in the public sector has consistently shown that public fairness perceptions are highly context‑dependent and that purely automated prioritisation often generates backlash. The CAIUS project, a multi‑year Volkswagen‑funded study on AI in urban societies, found that “semi‑automated decision‑making is favoured over fully automated approaches” and that algorithmic allocation can “reinforce existing structural disparities” when resources are scarce \citep{CAIUS2025}. More directly relevant, Moon and colleagues demonstrate the “paradox of prioritization in public sector algorithms”: even when prioritisation leads to mathematically efficient allocation, it “can intensify perceptions of inequality for impacted individuals” \citep{Moon2026}. This suggests that the Sequential Saturation strategy, while computationally optimal, may trigger perceptions of favouritism unless accompanied by transparent justification.

Conversely, empirical studies also show that the public does not reject algorithmic allocation outright. A study of public attitudes toward balancing equity and efficiency in resource allocation found “broad support for prioritizing equity, even at the cost of lower overall enrollment,” challenging the efficiency‑first paradigm \citep{DigitalGovHub2023}. This implies that citizens may accept unequal distribution if it is clearly framed as a pathway to eventual universal equity. In the Philippine context, research on social assistance programs such as the Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program (4Ps) and various “ayuda” schemes reveals that public cynicism toward targeting is driven less by the principle of unequal allocation and more by perceptions of “political favoritism and electoral manipulation” \citep{ManilaTimes2026}. Citizens distrust opaque, politically influenced allocation, not necessarily data‑driven, transparently justified targeting.

Thus, empirical evidence neither fully validates nor fully invalidates Pillar I. Instead, it suggests that the success of Sequential Saturation depends on three conditions that the simulation does not model: (1) transparent communication of the algorithm’s rationale, (2) evidence that the targeting is temporary and rotates, and (3) credible safeguards against political capture of the allocation process. These are not weaknesses of the model but requirements for its real‑world implementation.

\paragraph{Distinguishing Empirical Facts from Simulated Assumptions.}
A central requirement of interdisciplinary policy‑oriented modeling is clarity about what the simulation can and cannot claim. The following distinctions are therefore explicitly stated:

\emph{Empirically grounded facts} (derived from municipal documents, interviews, and PSA statistics):
\begin{itemize}
    \item The annual municipal SWM augmentation budget is fixed at P1.5 million (MENRO interview, Appendix B.1).
    \item Baseline household compliance is approximately \(12.5\%\) (municipal waste audit, Section 4.1.2).
    \item The Cost of Effort accounts for \(79\%\) of simulated compliance variance (Sobol GSA, Table 4.4).
    \item Barangay SWM budgets range from P15,000 (Babalaya) to P200,000 (Poblacion) (Table 4.1).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Simulated or assumed behavior} (model‑internal, not direct measurements):
\begin{itemize}
    \item The precise functional form of the household utility function (Equation 3.2) and the specific TPB weight values.
    \item The 70\% tipping point as a deterministic lock‑in threshold (Equation 3.4).
    \item The linear mapping from political capital to reward in the agent’s objective function (Equation 3.21).
    \item The “Sequential Saturation” strategy’s mathematical optimality – this is optimal \emph{within the model’s assumptions}, not a proven real‑world optimum.
\end{itemize}

Crucially, the simulation does \emph{not} predict that real citizens will behave exactly as the household agents do, nor that a real mayor would retain political capital exactly as the modelled political capital variable decays. The model’s value lies in demonstrating that, \emph{if} the behavioural assumptions hold, then Sequential Saturation outperforms static heuristics. Whether those assumptions hold in reality is a question for empirical pilot testing, not for the simulation.

\paragraph{Interdisciplinary Nature: Computational Modeling, Public Policy, and Governance.}
This study sits at the intersection of three disciplines, and its limitations and contributions must be understood in that light.

From \emph{computer science}, the contribution is methodological: the coupling of an Agent‑Based Model with Heuristic‑Guided Deep Reinforcement Learning to solve a high‑dimensional resource allocation problem under sparse rewards. The HuDRL architecture, action masking, and reward shaping are technical innovations applicable beyond waste management.

From \emph{public policy}, the contribution is prescriptive: the identification of “Sequential Saturation” as a policy logic that merits real‑world pilot testing. The simulation provides a rigorous, comparative evidence base for why equal distribution fails and why phased, concentrated allocation may succeed. However, the policy recommendation is conditional: it applies \emph{only if} the behavioural parameters used in the simulation approximate real community dynamics.

From \emph{governance}, the contribution is cautionary: the model highlights that technical optimality does not guarantee political feasibility. The empirical literature on algorithmic fairness, public trust, and Philippine patronage politics underscores that successful implementation requires transparency, participation, and safeguards against political capture \citep{TrustAIGov2025, CitizenPerceptionAI2025}. The DD‑SWM Framework’s first pillar is therefore not a purely technical prescription; it is a proposal to be implemented through accountable governance channels, with civil society oversight and iterative feedback.

\paragraph{Modeling Convenience vs. Reality.}
Several assumptions were made for computational convenience or to isolate specific mechanisms, not because they reflect full reality. These include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The 70\% tipping point as a hard lock‑in.} In reality, social norms are fuzzy thresholds, not deterministic switches. The model uses a hard lock‑in to simulate the “ratchet effect” without requiring complex path‑dependent variables.
    \item \textbf{No recycling of fines into the municipal budget.} The simulation does not reinvest collected penalties because forecasting political re‑allocation falls outside the predictive scope of this study.
    \item \textbf{Homogeneous household agents within income tiers.} Real households vary in dozens of unobserved ways; the model aggregates behaviour into a utility function for tractability.
    \item \textbf{No explicit informal economy.} Bribery and tipping of collection crews are not modelled, though interviews (Appendix B.6) suggest they exist.
\end{itemize}

These simplifications do not invalidate the comparative findings; they bound the claims. The model demonstrates what is possible under idealised conditions. Whether those conditions obtain in Bacolod is an empirical question for future pilot implementation.

\paragraph{Synthesis.}
Professor John’s concern about Pillar I is both valid and empirically supported: citizens react negatively to perceived unfairness, especially when allocation is opaque or politically captured. However, the empirical literature also shows that transparent, data‑driven, and temporary unequal allocation can be accepted when framed as a path to universal equity. The simulation cannot resolve this tension; it can only provide a rigorous optimisation baseline. The interdisciplinary contribution of this study is to show that technical optimality and political feasibility are distinct problems, and that the former is a necessary but insufficient condition for the latter. The DD‑SWM Framework is therefore offered not as a turnkey solution but as a testable hypothesis for LGU pilot programmes.

Traditional environmental policy design in developing nations frequently suffers from the “implementation deficit”—a phenomenon where ambitious national mandates fail at the local level due to a mismatch between legislative intent and actual local capacity \citep{Howlett2019, Magno2021}. To transition from heuristic‑based administration to predictive governance, this study proposes the \textbf{Data‑Driven Solid Waste Management (DD‑SWM) Framework}. This framework discards static, uniform budget allocations in favour of a dynamic, four‑pillar model: Algorithmic Resource Targeting, Friction Reduction, Adaptive Behavioral Mechanics, and Predictive Prototyping.

\section{Pillar I: Algorithmic Resource Targeting}
\label{sec:pillar1}

The first pillar of the proposed framework addresses the critical structural flaw of “Equal Distribution,” frequently referred to in public administration as the “peanut‑buttering” effect. Traditional administrative heuristics—wherein funds are spread evenly across all barangays—are often employed by local executives to maintain a veneer of political neutrality and avoid accusations of favouritism \citep{Cruz2020}. However, within the context of this simulation, such an approach is mathematically modelled as a mechanism of dilution \citep{Alphonso2024}. By distributing a limited budget equally across jurisdictions with vastly different needs, the Local Government Unit (LGU) ensures that the critical density of enforcement or incentives required to trigger behavioural change is never achieved in densely populated, highly resistant urban centres like Barangay Poblacion \citep{Villanueva2021}.

To resolve this dilution, the LGU must adopt a strategy of “Sequential Saturation,” which requires reframing the budget allocation process as a structured, phased rollout. The Mayor and the Municipal Environment and Natural Resources Office (MENRO) should justify concentrating the overwhelming majority of the municipal MOOE budget into the most resistant zones, such as Poblacion, during the initial phase. By treating high‑density areas as “Pilot Zones,” the LGU can break the initial behavioural inertia and lock in social norms via the Social Norm Shield before rotating those resources to rural areas like Demologan \citep{Zheng2022b}. This approach aligns with the “focal point” theory of resource allocation, which posits that overwhelming a single node in a network creates positive spillover effects for adjacent jurisdictions \citep{Ostrom2010}.

Complementing this geographic concentration is the necessity for dynamic financial restructuring. The simulation data revealed severe fiscal disparities, with some jurisdictions operating on SWM budgets as low as P15,000 annually. To achieve municipal‑wide sustainability, the LGU must abandon static, historical appropriation ordinances in favour of a pooled resource model. By cross‑subsidising high‑impact infrastructure in underfunded barangays based on quarterly algorithmic triage, the municipal government ensures that capital flows precisely where behavioural resistance is highest \citep{Abdallah2021, Turchetta2019}. Operationally, this transition requires the drafting of a Sangguniang Bayan (SB) Resolution to legally ring‑fence funds for targeted saturation. In this administrative context, the computational model serves as a “mathematical shield” for the executive branch, depoliticising the allocation process by providing a data‑backed rationale for abandoning simultaneous equality in favour of systemic efficacy \citep{Nishimura2022}.

\section{Pillar II: Friction Reduction and Infrastructure}
\label{sec:pillar2}

The second pillar focuses on the physical environment and choice architecture, grounded in the results of the Global Sensitivity Analysis. The GSA confirmed that the “Cost of Effort” (\(c_{\text{effort}}\)) is the dominant inhibitor of compliance in Bacolod, accounting for approximately 79\% of total output variance \citep{Yazawa2025}. In contrast, the near‑zero sensitivity of the “Attitude” parameter quantifies a distinct “Value‑Action Gap,” wherein citizens may possess high environmental awareness yet fail to act due to systemic physical barriers \citep{Blake1999, Zhao2022}. These findings fundamentally indicate that passive Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) campaigns, such as flyers or radio broadcasts, will yield negligible returns if the underlying “sludge” or physical friction of segregation remains unaddressed \citep{Sunstein2019, Moeini2023}.

Consequently, the LGU must pivot its fiscal priorities from awareness generation to the enhancement of physical convenience. A primary intervention involves the direct subsidisation of segregation receptacles in low‑income zones. Interviews revealed that residents in economically vulnerable areas, such as Liangan East, often find even basic materials like sacks (\textit{sako}) prohibitively expensive. By relying on individual procurement, the LGU effectively penalises the poor for non‑compliance. Allocating funds to distribute free, standardised segregation bins to these households neutralises the financial barrier to entry and simplifies the decision‑making process for the agent \citep{Thaler2008}.

In addition to receptacle subsidies, the LGU must prioritise the resolution of logistical bottlenecks that force residents toward undesirable practices like illegal dumping or the use of unmanaged compost pits \citep{Premakumara2014}. This includes the urgent need for hardware maintenance, exemplified by the currently non‑functional MultiCab in Barangay Liangan East. Ensuring that the physical act of waste disposal aligns seamlessly with the desired civic behaviour is a prerequisite for any punitive measure. Within this framework, the LGU acknowledges that it cannot ethically or practically penalise a populace for failing to utilise infrastructure that is either non‑functional or non‑existent. By reducing \(c_{\text{effort}}\) through direct physical support, the municipality allows the AI’s optimised policy mix to take root in an environment designed for success.

\section{Pillar III: Adaptive Behavioral Mechanics}
\label{sec:pillar3}

The third pillar governs how the LGU applies pressure and rewards over time, moving away from rigid, permanent enforcement structures toward adaptive behavioural interventions.

\subsection{Calibrated Enforcement and Political Capital}

The simulation provides strong computational evidence that a “Pure Enforcement” strategy is politically brittle, triggering a “Political Collapse” by Quarter 2 due to extreme public backlash. Heavy‑handed penalisation without adequate infrastructure or prior behavioural conditioning traps the LGU in an unsustainable cycle of resentment, often resulting in electoral punishment for incumbent officials \citep{Weaver1986}. 

The proposed framework advises against uniform, draconian ticketing. Instead, MENRO should deploy citation tickets selectively during the initial “Saturation Phase” of a targeted barangay to shatter early resistance. Once compliance begins to rise, the LGU must immediately scale back punitive measures. This “pulsed enforcement” creates a psychological deterrent without exhausting municipal goodwill, allowing Political Capital to recover to a stable 1.0000 \citep{Ayres1992}.

\subsection{The Graduation Rule and the 70\% Tipping Point}

The HuDRL agent successfully discovered that civic behaviour does not require perpetual, high‑intensity municipal funding once it becomes culturally normalised. Complex contagion models suggest that once a critical mass adopts a behaviour, the behaviour becomes self‑sustaining \citep{Centola2018}. The LGU must continuously monitor barangays until they cross this “Social Tipping Point,” computationally identified in this study as approximately 70\% compliance.

Once a barangay breaches this threshold, peer pressure and internalised social norms (\(\omega_{sn}\)) naturally sustain the segregation behaviour. The framework introduces the “Graduation Rule”: upon reaching 70\% compliance, MENRO must drastically titrate that barangay’s funding down to a fractional “Maintenance Budget.” This budget is tuned solely to counter habit decay, freeing up massive fiscal capital to besiege the next resistant population centre.

\section{Pillar IV: Predictive Policy Prototyping}
\label{sec:pillar4}

The final pillar cements the framework by providing the LGU with the computational tools required to sustain it. Traditionally, LGU appropriation ordinances are drafted based on static heuristics, historical precedent, and political intuition. This highlights the potential benefits of transitioning to a dynamic, “What‑If” planning model, effectively introducing the concept of a “Digital Twin” for municipal public policy \citep{Batty2018}.

The “Bacolod Multi‑View Simulation UI” developed in this study serves as a proof‑of‑concept for this pillar. While the current iteration is a research prototype, it demonstrates the profound utility of predictive modelling in public administration. By commissioning or internally developing localised decision‑support systems based on this DRL architecture, future administrators will be able to input demographic shifts, vehicle logistics constraints, and shifting budget allocations to simulate policy outcomes \textit{before} enacting municipal ordinances \citep{Kitchin2014}. This minimises the risk of unintended consequences, avoids the costly misallocation of public funds, and permanently shifts the LGU from a paradigm of reactive enforcement to one of proactive, evidence‑based governance.
