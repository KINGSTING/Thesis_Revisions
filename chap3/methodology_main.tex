\chapter{Methodology}

This chapter details the methodological framework constructed to address the identified research gap. To operationalize the study's objective—optimizing dynamic policy under budgetary constraints—this research employed a coupled Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) framework. This integrated approach was specifically chosen for its unique capacity to simulate a complex social system of household compliance while simultaneously modeling the Local Government Unit (LGU) as an adaptive, learning agent. Unlike traditional static scenario analysis, this framework allowed the governing agent to autonomously discover optimal resource allocation strategies over time. This chapter first presents the overall research design, followed by a detailed description of the model's components: the ABM environment and the DRL agent. It then outlines the procedures for model parameterization, validation, and the simulation experiments conducted to generate and evaluate the adaptive policies.

\section{Research Design}

This study employed a computational simulation research design that integrated Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) with Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) optimization. This design created a virtual laboratory for testing Solid Waste Management (SWM) policies, allowing for the autonomous discovery of the optimal resource allocation strategy without the cost and risk of real-world trials. The research followed three main phases: (1) Model parameterization using literature synthesis, (2) DRL integration and training, and (3) Policy scenario simulation and analysis.

\section{Data Synthesis and Parameter Estimation}

This study did not collect new, large-scale primary survey data (such as a household-level census). Instead, it constructed a high-fidelity model by synthesizing data from three key secondary and existing sources: (1) academic literature, (2) public government statistics, (3) operational and qualitative data already gathered from LGU records and the key-informant interviews presented in Appendix \ref{appendix:menro_interview}, and (4) cost parameter estimation.

\subsection{Behavioral Parameters from Literature}

The foundational psychological parameters for the household agents' Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) utility function—specifically the behavioral weights for Attitude ($w_A$), Subjective Norms ($w_{SN}$), and Perceived Behavioral Control ($w_{PBC}$)—were derived through a systematic review and meta-synthesis of existing academic studies on Solid Waste Management (SWM) and pro-environmental behavior \citep{Taraghi2025, Moeini2023}. This approach ensured the model's behavioral core was grounded in empirical evidence.

To ensure the simulation possessed high ecological validity, the initialization of Household Agent behavioral parameters was grounded in empirical Knowledge, Attitude, and Practices (KAP) data derived from the recent study by \citet{Paigalan2025}. Although the source study characterized the KAP profile of riverside barangays in Northern Mindanao, this dataset served as a robust and high-fidelity proxy for the coastal Municipality of Bacolod due to the shared socio-economic and cultural context of the region.

Crucially, the agents were not instantiated as \textit{tabula rasa} entities; rather, the initialization logic was rigorously designed to replicate the ``Intention-Action Gap'' frequently observed in developing economies \citep{Yazawa2025, Catiil2025}. As detailed in Table \ref{tab:initialization_parameters}, agents were initialized with a relatively high Attitude ($A_0 \approx 0.66$) contrasted with a significantly lower Baseline Compliance ($B_0 \approx 0.58$). This specific parameterization compelled the Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent to discover policy interventions capable of bridging this behavioral dissonance, rather than merely addressing a theoretical lack of awareness \citep{Paigalan2025}.

\subsection{Socio-demographic and Operational Parameters}

The Agent-Based Model (ABM) was explicitly contextualized to the seven barangays from which the Local Government Unit (LGU) collected waste: Liangan East, Esperanza, Poblacion, Binuni, Demologan, Mati, and Babalaya. A crucial methodological step involved rigorously parameterizing each of these seven local communities as a unique, high-fidelity environment within the model \citep{Jimenez2025, Brugiere2022}. Data collection for this parameterization was conducted through key-informant interviews with officials from all seven target barangays, gathering essential operational data that included local population and household counts, the existing local Solid Waste Management (SWM) budget and resource allocation, local staff levels (such as Barangay Officials and BPATs/Tanods) available for implementation, current compliance estimates, and specific local SWM challenges \citep{Villanueva2021, Florida2023}.

The data already secured from Barangay Liangan East (refer to Appendix \ref{appendix:liangan_interview}), detailing its 608 households, 32 staff, and \textpeso 30,000 local SWM budget, served as the initial prototype for building and calibrating the generic \texttt{BarangayAgent} class. Following this prototype development, the model was expanded as data from the other six planned interviews was collected, with six additional, unique \texttt{BarangayAgent} environments instantiated, each loaded with its own precise operational parameters. Furthermore, a socio-demographic baseline, specifically household income profiles and population densities for all seven barangays, was established using publicly available Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) data \citep{Abordo2025}, which was then validated and refined by the qualitative insights and local context gathered from the interview process.

\subsection{Policy and Operational Parameters}

The strategic decision-making process of the LGU-DRL agent is fundamentally governed by the fiscal constraints identified during the institutional audit. Specifically, the agent operates within a fixed annual Solid Waste Management (SWM) budget of \textpeso 1,500,000 (refer to Appendix \ref{appendix:menro_interview}). To simulate realistic administrative cycles and allow for behavioral adaptation among household agents, the simulation employs a quarterly decision-making frequency. Consequently, the annual appropriation is discretized into a quarterly operating budget ($B_q$) of \textpeso 375,000. This temporal structuring allows the Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent to observe the immediate socio-behavioral shifts resulting from a specific policy mix and adjust the subsequent allocation to maximize long-term compliance rewards.

The LGU agent’s action space is defined by a continuous allocation vector across three primary policy levers, each targeting a different construct of the household utility function. First, Monetary Incentives are modeled as direct subsidies (e.g., material-recovery exchange programs) designed to enhance the economic utility of compliance. Second, Enforcement expenditures facilitate the deployment of "Eco-warriors" and inspectors; this lever functions by increasing the probability of detection and the perceived severity of non-compliance, thereby influencing the agent's normative and calculative behavior. Finally, Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) Campaigns utilize funds for media dissemination and community workshops to improve the household agents' Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) scores. By formalizing these levers as competing financial priorities, the model allows the DRL agent to discover the optimal "Policy Mix"—the specific ratio of incentives, penalties, and education—that yields the highest compliance rate while strictly adhering to the municipal budget.

\subsection{Cost Parameter Estimation}

To operationalize the continuous resource allocation model, the study defined specific cost functions to quantify the depletion of the \textpeso 1,500,000 annual municipal fund. These functions transformed abstract policy decisions into concrete financial constraints, addressing the chronic lack of funding often cited as a cause of institutional failure \citep{Ibanez2022, Santos2025}.

\subsubsection{Cost of Enforcement ($C_{\mathrm{Enf}}$)}

Enforcement expenditure was calculated as a function of the Coverage Ratio. Based on the logistical constraints of the Municipality of Bacolod, which included dispersed coastal and inland barangays, the model assumed a single enforcement officer could effectively monitor a maximum of 30 households per day. This conservative estimate accounted for travel time between households and the administrative burden of issuing citations \citep{Nishimura2022}.

\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{Enf}} = (N_{\mathrm{Enforcers}} \times W_{\mathrm{Daily}} \times 66)
    \label{eq:cost_enforcement}
\end{equation}

\noindent Where $N_{\mathrm{Enforcers}}$ was the number of personnel required, $W_{\mathrm{Daily}}$ was the regional minimum daily wage for Region X, and 66 represented the working days in a quarter. The Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent’s budgetary allocation determined $N_{\mathrm{Enforcers}}$, which subsequently defined the Probability of Detection ($P_{\mathrm{Detect}}$) variable in the household utility function \citep{MuZhang2021}.

\subsubsection{Cost of Incentives ($C_{\mathrm{Inc}}$)}

Incentive costs were modeled as a Variable Success Function, creating a dynamic positive feedback loop where successful behavioral change increased financial liability.

\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{Inc}} = (V_{\mathrm{Reward}} \times N_{\mathrm{Compliant}})
    \label{eq:cost_incentives}
\end{equation}

\noindent Where $V_{\mathrm{Reward}}$ was the monetary value of the incentive and $N_{\mathrm{Compliant}}$ was the count of compliant households. This structure introduced a critical "Victim of Success" risk; the DRL agent had to learn to optimize for a compliance equilibrium that was fiscally sustainable, avoiding scenarios where high compliance rapidly depleted the municipal treasury \citep{WorldBank2022}.

\subsubsection{Cost of Information \& Educational Campaign ($C_{\mathrm{IEC}}$)}

Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) costs were modeled as Tiered Fixed Costs, reflecting the discrete nature of media procurement.

\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{IEC}} = (N_{\mathrm{Spots}} \times R_{\mathrm{Radio}}) + (N_{\mathrm{Events}} \times C_{\mathrm{Mobilization}})
    \label{eq:cost_iec}
\end{equation}

\noindent This formulation aligned with the LGU’s operational reliance on local radio broadcasting (e.g., 101.3 Grace Covenant FM) as the primary dissemination channel for environmental policy \citep{Collado2024}.

\section{Multi-Level Agent-Based Model Development}

The model was developed in an existing Agent-Based Modelling Software Python library called \textit{MESA} \citep{Mesa2025}, a methodological choice strongly supported by recent reviews of computational tools in solid waste management \citep{TianReview2024, Ma2023}. Its architecture consisted of one single ABM environment that contained 7 distinct \texttt{BarangayAgent} objects, which in turn contained their respective populations of \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects.

The simulation utilized a single, discrete ABM environment to contain its operational entities and manage its temporal structure. Time progression was segmented into recurring quarterly loops, establishing the fundamental unit of analysis and adaptation for the governance agents. The architecture was strictly hierarchical: it contained seven distinct \texttt{BarangayAgent} objects in the upper layer, which encapsulated their respective populations of \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects in the lower layer. This multi-level approach was essential for accurately capturing the flow of mandates and the heterogeneity between different governance units in socio-environmental systems \citep{Brugiere2022}.

The \textit{BarangayAgents} embodied the Local Government Unit (LGU) implementation layer, responsible for fiscal management (fund allocation), policy formulation and implementation (e.g., establishing waste segregation mandates), and personnel management (deployment of enforcement staff). This aligned with the decentralized governance structure where local units were the primary implementers of environmental law \citep{Nishimura2022}. The \textit{BarangayAgents} were the key recipients of the feedback mechanism, utilizing the aggregated compliance rate as an observational State and a prescriptive Reward signal for its internal Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) mechanism \citep{Jimenez2025}.

The \textit{EnforcementAgents} functioned as the operational execution arm of the LGU, tasked with the stochastic monitoring of compliance. Constrained by the budget allocation determined by the \textit{BarangayAgents}, these agents conducted random inspections to verify waste segregation at the source, effectively operationalizing the "No Segregation, No Collection" policy \citep{Badua2022}. Their presence directly manipulated the "perceived intensity of enforcement" variable within the simulation, transforming abstract policy mandates into tangible risks for non-compliant households \citep{Carpio2025}. This agent layer introduced the critical dynamic of detection probability: while higher enforcement density significantly deterred non-compliance ("green criminology"), it incurred higher operational costs, forcing the system to balance fiscal sustainability with strict environmental policing \citep{Chen2023, Ibanez2022}.

The \textit{HouseholdAgents} represented the citizenry and were geographically and administratively segregated by their assigned barangay. Household behavior was modeled through a process of rational bounded observation, where agents assessed three primary exogenous factors: the clarity and strictness of LGU policies, the perceived intensity of local enforcement (driven by the \textit{EnforcementAgents}), and the compliance behavior of neighboring households (social norms) \citep{Meng2018, Liao2024}. This assessment culminated in an independent segregation decision, which dictated the agent's contribution to the collective compliance level. The quarterly feedback loop aggregated these decisions into seven discrete \textit{BarangayComplianceRate} values, which were then transmitted back to the LGU-DRL Agent, closing the adaptive system loop and facilitating the study of policy response to citizen behavior \citep{Ceschi2021, Fontaine2024}.

\subsection{Formalization as a Markov Decision Process}

To facilitate the autonomous discovery of optimal strategies in the absence of longitudinal historical data, the interaction between the Local Government Unit (LGU) and the household agents was formalized as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP). This mathematical framework transitioned the Agent-Based Model from a descriptive simulation into a prescriptive optimization environment, where the LGU-DRL agent iteratively interacted with a stochastic state space to maximize a cumulative utility function \citep{Kompella2020}. The MDP is formally defined by the quintuple $\mathcal{M} = \langle S, A, P, R, \gamma \rangle$, representing the state space, action space, transition probability, reward signal, and discount factor, respectively.

The State Space ($S$) is characterized as a multi-dimensional, continuous vector $S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$, which provides the agent with a comprehensive snapshot of the municipal environment at each decision epoch. This vector encompasses the mean compliance rates across the seven constituent barangays, the normalized remaining budget for the fiscal year, and the simulation time index to account for seasonal or temporal variations in waste generation. By including these macroscopic variables, the agent can perceive the heterogeneous social landscape of the municipality, allowing for localized rather than monolithic policy responses \citep{HaMinh2025}. Correspondingly, the Action Space ($A$) is defined as a continuous vector $A_t \in \mathbb{R}^{21}$. This high-dimensional space represents the granular allocation of the quarterly budget across the three primary policy levers—Enforcement, Incentives, and Information Education Campaigns (IEC)—for each individual barangay. The choice of a continuous action space over a discrete one is critical for achieving "policy precision," as it enables the Reinforcement Learning agent to fine-tune resource intensity to the specific tipping points of household behavioral change \citep{Rajesh2025}.

The system dynamics are governed by the Transition Function ($P$), denoted as $P(S_{t+1} | S_t, A_t)$. Within this framework, the ABM serves as the implicit transition engine; unlike classical control problems where transitions are defined by explicit differential equations, the state evolution here emerges from the aggregate, non-linear behaviors of heterogeneous household agents updating their internal utility based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). Consequently, the simulation acts as a generative model where the subsequent state $S_{t+1}$ is a stochastic manifestation of the community’s collective response to the LGU's policy intervention $A_t$ \citep{TianReview2024, Jimenez2025}. To evaluate the efficacy of these interventions, the Reward Function ($R$) provides a scalar feedback signal $R_t$ that balances the LGU’s multi-objective mandates. This function is designed to reward high aggregate compliance while penalizing fiscal overspending and the social friction associated with excessive punitive enforcement \citep{Dey2025}. Finally, a Discount Factor of $\gamma = 0.99$ is applied to the objective function, ensuring that the DRL agent prioritizes strategies that foster long-term, sustained behavioral shifts rather than pursuing ephemeral, short-term compliance spikes that may deplete the annual budget prematurely.

\subsection{Household Agent Design}

The decision-making architecture of the \texttt{HouseholdAgent} is governed by a dynamic utility function grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which posits that an individual's intention to perform a behavior is a function of their attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control \citep{Ceschi2021}. In contrast to static behavioral models, this framework incorporates time-variant weights ($w(t)$) for psychological constructs, allowing agent behavior to evolve non-linearly in response to LGU interventions and social feedback loops \citep{Taraghi2025, Ma2023}. The core decision logic is represented by the utility of segregation ($U_{\mathrm{segregate}}$):

\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{segregate}} = (w_A(t) A) + (w_{SN}(t) SN_{\mathrm{local}}) + (w_{PBC}(t) PBC_{\mathrm{infra}}) - C_{\mathrm{Net}} + \epsilon
    \label{eq:utility_function}
\end{equation}

In this formulation, $w_A(t)$ represents the temporal evolution of the agent's internal valuation of segregation, functioning as a decay model that increases in response to Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) investment and decays stochastically in the absence of reinforcement to simulate ``public forgetting'' \citep{Trushna2024}. The social component, $SN_{\mathrm{local}}$, is an endogenous variable derived from the observed compliance rate of the agent's immediate spatial neighborhood (radius $r$), capturing the effects of social pressure and observational learning \citep{Meng2018, Liao2024}. To account for the inherent uncertainty in human decision-making, the term $\epsilon$ introduces stochastic noise, ensuring the model reflects real-world behavioral variance \citep{Subedi2025}.

A critical innovation in this model is the calculation of $C_{\mathrm{Net}}$, the net behavioral cost, which characterizes the perceived friction of compliance. This variable is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{Net}} = C_{\mathrm{Effort}} + (\gamma C_{\mathrm{Monetary}}) - (\gamma I) - (\gamma F P_{\mathrm{Detection}})
    \label{eq:net_disutility}
\end{equation}

\noindent This cost-benefit sub-model integrates both physical and financial barriers, defined as follows:

\begin{description}
    \item[$\bm{C_{\mathrm{Effort}}}$:] The physical hassle associated with the washing, sorting, and storage of waste.
    \item[$\bm{C_{\mathrm{Monetary}}}$:] Tangible financial expenses, such as the procurement of color-coded bins or sacks.
    \item[$\bm{\gamma}$ (Income Sensitivity):] A weighting factor derived from household income level; for lower-income households, $\gamma > 1$ as financial levers ($I$ and $F$) carry greater psychological weight, whereas $\gamma < 1$ for higher-income households.
    \item[$\bm{I}$ and $\bm{F}$:] The objective magnitudes of monetary incentives and punitive fines, respectively.
    \item[$\bm{P_{\mathrm{Detection}}}$:] The likelihood of enforcement detection, which is dynamically linked to the LGU's resource allocation for enforcement personnel.
\end{description}

Furthermore, the model captures complex psychological feedbacks such as ``psychological reactance.'' While LGU investments in IEC campaigns generally improve Attitude ($A$) and Subjective Norms ($SN$) \citep{Trushna2024}, the model stipulates that if enforcement intensity crosses a specific threshold, Attitude ($A$) may paradoxically decrease. This inverse reaction reflects the tendency of individuals to resist coercive mandates when they perceive a loss of autonomy, a concept central to Nudge Theory applications in waste management \citep{LoanBalanay2023}. Finally, Perceived Behavioral Control ($PBC$) is calibrated by the availability of barangay-level infrastructure, ensuring the simulation acknowledges that even highly motivated agents may fail to comply if functional bins or Materials Recovery Facilities (MRFs) are absent.

\subsection{Behavioral Hysteresis and the Internalization of Social Norms}

While the baseline \texttt{HouseholdAgent} architecture operates on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), standard models often exhibit unrealistic behavioral decay where compliance collapses immediately upon the cessation of external stimuli. To more accurately simulate the real-world stability of established habits, this study incorporates a \textit{Norm Internalization Mechanism}. This mechanism addresses the phenomenon of ``Cultural Inertia,'' where a behavior transitions from a calculated, incentive-driven decision to an internalized social habit once a critical tipping point is reached \citep{Ceschi2021}.

In this framework, the standard attitude update function, which typically suffers from a decay constant ($\delta$) due to enforcement fatigue or apathy, is refined by a dynamic damping factor ($D_{\mathrm{factor}}$):

\begin{equation}
    A_{t+1} = A_t - (\delta \times D_{\mathrm{factor}})
\end{equation}

The damping factor is governed by the strength of local social norms ($SN$), representing the protective effect of community-wide compliance. Specifically, the model identifies a \textit{tipping point at 70\% compliance}, beyond which the behavior is considered internalized:

\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{factor}} = 
    \begin{cases} 
        0.1 & \text{if } SN > 0.70 \text{ (Strong Norms / Internalized)} \\
        0.5 & \text{if } SN > 0.50 \text{ (Moderate Norms)} \\
        1.0 & \text{otherwise (Weak Norms)}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Furthermore, to prevent the total erosion of community standards during periods of fiscal austerity or low LGU intervention, the model introduces a \textit{Social Norm Floor set at 40\%}. This floor ensures that once a barangay has achieved a baseline level of awareness, the perceived social pressure does not drop to zero, representing the residual collective memory and existing communal infrastructure of the municipality. This refinement is critical for addressing the sustainability objectives of this research, as it allows the Deep Reinforcement Learning agent to discover strategies that foster self-sustaining compliance rather than perpetual reliance on high-cost monetary incentives.

\subsection{Barangay Agent Design}

The \textit{BarangayAgent} serves as the critical intermediate implementation layer, bridging the gap between municipal-level resource allocation and household-level behavioral shifts. The model initializes seven unique instances of this agent class—representing the geographically and socioeconomically distinct units of Liangan East, Ezperanza, Poblacion, Binuni, Demologan, Mati, and Babalaya—calibrated with parameters derived from municipal institutional audits. The utilization of Agent-Based Modeling to simulate the dynamics between governance units and neighborhood interactions is a robust approach for evaluating public policy efficacy in complex social systems. Within this framework, the \textit{BarangayAgent} functions as an executive entity that manages the "stacking effect" of fiscal resources, combining the quarterly augmentation funds provided by the LGU-DRL agent with its own localized, baseline operating budget, such as the \textpeso 30,000 appropriation for Barangay Liangan East.

Beyond fiscal management, the agent serves as the primary mediator of environmental policy by operationalizing the "No Segregation, No Collection" mandate through the deployment of local enforcement staff and "Eco-warriors". This operational capacity is formally linked to the individual household's perceived probability of detection ($P_{\mathrm{Detection}}$), as the intensity of local enforcement is directly proportional to the agent's allocated enforcement fund. At the conclusion of each simulated quarter, the \textit{BarangayAgent} tracks and reports its aggregate \textit{BarangayComplianceRate} to the LGU agent. This creates a high-fidelity feedback loop where local citizen behavior informs subsequent municipal-level policy adjustments, supporting an adaptive policy analysis framework that accounts for the heterogeneity of the municipality’s constituent barangays.

\section{Heuristic-Guided Deep Reinforcement Learning}

To address the high-dimensional resource allocation problem inherent in municipal budgeting, this study develops a \textbf{Heuristic-Guided Deep Reinforcement Learning (HuDRL)} framework. This architecture utilizes Deep Proximal Policy Optimization (Deep PPO) to approximate an optimal governance policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ within a continuous action space.

\subsection{Addressing Reward Sparsity}

A fundamental challenge in applying Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) to complex governance systems is the \textit{Sparse Reward Problem}. The resource allocation task involves a high-dimensional continuous action space ($d=21$), representing the simultaneous adjustment of three policy levers (IEC, Enforcement, Incentives) across seven distinct barangays. 

In a standard, unguided Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) environment, an agent effectively wanders randomly, rarely discovering that concentrating massive resources on a single barangay (the "Sequential Saturation" strategy) is the key to breaking behavioral inertia. Instead, agents frequently converge to a "Status Quo" local optimum—a safe but sub-optimal strategy characterized by thin, equitable resource distribution that fails to trigger behavioral tipping points. To overcome this, the \textit{HuDRL framework} integrates domain-specific heuristics to guide exploration toward economically and physically viable strategies.

\subsection{HuDRL Implementation}

To operationalize the proposed framework, the HuDRL agent was implemented as a specialized policy optimization module integrated directly with the MESA-based simulation environment. Unlike off-the-shelf implementations, this study developed a custom Actor-Critic network explicitly designed to process the multi-modal state vector of the municipality—combining fiscal, social, and temporal data—into a unified policy output. The following subsections detail the technical specifications of the agent, including the neural network topology, the mathematical formulation of the state-action spaces, and the specific normalization techniques employed to strictly enforce the municipality's fiscal constraints.

\subsubsection{Neural Network Architecture}
The agent is constructed using an Actor-Critic architecture, where two separate deep neural networks—the Actor (policy) and the Critic (value)—work in tandem to optimize decision-making. The input layer accepts a normalized state vector $S_t$ containing critical observations, including barangay compliance rates, remaining budget status, and political capital. To process this high-dimensional data, both networks utilize two fully connected (dense) hidden layers with 64 neurons each. These layers employ Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation functions, a specific architectural choice intended to capture the non-linear and complex relationships between enforcement intensity and household behavioral responses \citep{Dey2025}.

The network culminates in a specialized output layer that governs the agent's continuous action space. Unlike standard regression outputs, the Actor network utilizes a Softmax activation function to generate the final policy distribution. This mechanism transforms the raw neural logits into a normalized probability distribution, mathematically guaranteeing that the sum of all budget allocations across the 21 policy levers equals exactly 100\% of the available quarterly fund \citep{Jimenez2025}. This constraint is fundamental to the model's validity, ensuring that every generated policy remains fiscally feasible within the municipality's hard budgetary limits.

\subsection{RL State Representation \texorpdfstring{($S_t$)}{(St)}}

The state space $S_t$ constitutes the comprehensive observation vector accessible to the LGU agent at the onset of each decision epoch $t$. This high-dimensional vector serves as the cognitive interface between the agent and the environment, enabling the mapping of fiscal actions to longitudinal social outcomes. By encoding the system's socio-economic status, the state representation captures the complexity inherent in decentralized governance \citep{Jimenez2025}.

The state vector is formalized as:

\begin{equation}
    S_t = [\mathbf{CB}_{1...7}, B_{\mathrm{Rem}}, M_{\mathrm{Index}}, P_{\mathrm{Cap}}]
    \label{eq:state_vector}
\end{equation}

\noindent Each component plays a distinct strategic role in the optimization process:

\begin{description}
    \item[$\mathbf{CB}_{1...7}$ (Barangay Compliance Vector):] A 7-dimensional continuous sub-vector representing the real-time segregation compliance rate for each constituent barangay. Tracking spatially disaggregated performance allows the agent to identify heterogeneity in household behavior, facilitating targeted interventions toward underperforming units rather than inefficient blanket policies \citep{Villanueva2021}.
    
    \item[$B_{\mathrm{Rem}}$ (Fiscal Liquidity):] The scalar value representing the remaining balance of the annual fund. This variable provides the necessary fiscal context for "pacing" strategies, allowing the agent to solve the multi-objective optimization problem without violating hard budgetary constraints \citep{Abdallah2021, Torkayesh2021}.
    
    \item[$M_{\mathrm{Index}}$ (Temporal Horizon):] A discrete integer representing the current fiscal quarter ($t \in \{1,2,3,4\}$). This index establishes the finite horizon of the Markov Decision Process (MDP), ensuring the agent optimizes spending relative to the remaining time in the fiscal year \citep{Akbarpour2021}.
    
    \item[$P_{\mathrm{Cap}}$ (Political Capital):] A normalized scalar ($0 \le P \le 1$) representing the populace's accumulated trust or tolerance for governance. This variable acts as a dynamic constraint on the admissibility of punitive measures, operationalizing the principle that effective enforcement requires social acceptability \citep{Nishimura2022}.
\end{description}

The dynamics of Political Capital are modeled to reflect the tension between enforcement intensity and public sentiment. $P_{\mathrm{Cap}}$ evolves according to the following transition function:

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{Cap}(t+1)} = P_{\mathrm{Cap}(t)} - (\alpha \cdot E_{\mathrm{Intensity}}) + (\beta \cdot T_{\mathrm{Decay}})
    \label{eq:political_capital}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\alpha$ denotes the \textit{sensitivity coefficient}, representing the rate of trust depletion relative to the normalized enforcement intensity ($E_{\mathrm{Intensity}}$), and $\beta$ represents the \textit{natural recovery rate}, characterizing the restoration of political capital over time in the absence of coercive interventions.

\subsection{RL Action Space \texorpdfstring{($A_t$)}{(At)} and Budget Normalization}

The action space ($A_t$) constitutes the feasible set of governance decisions executable by the LGU agent at any given time step. In contrast to discrete control tasks that select from a finite menu of options, this study employs a high-dimensional \textbf{Continuous Action Space} ($A_t \in \mathbb{R}^{21}$). This vector represents the simultaneous allocation of resources across three distinct policy levers—Information, Education, and Communication (IEC), Enforcement, and Monetary Incentives—independently applied to each of the seven constituent barangays:

\begin{equation}
    A_t = [\mathbf{Alloc}_{\mathrm{IEC}, B_{1..7}}, \mathbf{Alloc}_{\mathrm{Enf}, B_{1..7}}, \mathbf{Alloc}_{\mathrm{Inc}, B_{1..7}}]
    \label{eq:action_space}
\end{equation}

A critical constraint in municipal governance is the finite nature of financial resources. Unlike standard reinforcement learning tasks where action outputs may be independent, municipal budgeting is inherently a zero-sum game. To enforce fiscal viability directly within the model architecture, the raw output of the neural network ($A_{\mathrm{raw}}$) is processed through a \textbf{Softmax Normalization Layer}. This operation transforms the raw logits into a probability distribution, which is subsequently scaled by the Quarterly Budget Cap ($B_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}$):

\begin{equation}
   A_{\mathrm{final}} = \mathrm{Softmax}(A_{\mathrm{raw}}) \cdot B_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}
   \label{eq:softmax_constraint}
\end{equation}

This transformation provides a mathematical guarantee that the summation of all allocated funds across the 21 channels adheres strictly to the hard budgetary constraint:

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^{21} A_{t,i} \le B_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}
    \label{eq:fiscal_constraint}
\end{equation}

By constraining the action space to $B_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}$ (derived from the \textpeso 1,500,000 annual appropriation divided by four quarters), the model compels the agent to optimize for cost-effectiveness within a sustainable, recurring fiscal framework rather than accessing the entirety of the annual fund in a single epoch \citep{Abdallah2021}.

\subsection{Multi-Objective Heuristic Reward Function}

The learning process of the LGU agent is driven by a Composite Reward Function ($R_{\mathrm{total}}$), designed to balance competing municipal priorities. While the primary goal is environmental compliance, the agent must navigate fiscal constraints and social acceptability to ensure policy longevity. 

To bridge the gap between immediate actions and delayed behavioral outcomes, the study employs \textbf{Reward Shaping}, which provides the agent with denser feedback signals during the training phase. The total reward at each time step $t$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{total}} = w_1 R_{\mathrm{Comp}} + w_2 R_{\mathrm{Sustain}} - w_3 P_{\mathrm{Backlash}} + R_{\mathrm{Shaping}}
    \label{eq:total_reward}
\end{equation}

The components of the reward function are defined as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compliance Objective ($R_{\mathrm{Comp}}$):} The primary environmental target, calculated as the population-weighted average segregation rate across all seven barangays.
    
    \item \textbf{Sustainability Regularization ($R_{\mathrm{Sustain}}$):} This term enforces fiscal stability by penalizing the agent for deviating from an ideal "burn rate." This ensures the annual budget is not prematurely exhausted:
    \begin{equation}
        R_{\mathrm{Sustain}} = - \left| \frac{S_{\mathrm{Actual}}}{B_{\mathrm{Total}}} - \frac{1}{12} \right|
    \end{equation}
    where $S_{\mathrm{Actual}}$ is the current spending and $B_{\mathrm{Total}}$ is the total annual fund.
    
    \item \textbf{Political Backlash Penalty ($P_{\mathrm{Backlash}}$):} A non-linear penalty triggered when Enforcement Intensity is high while compliance remains low. This reflects the reality that "penalizing all households is unfeasible" \citep{Nishimura2022}, forcing the agent to prioritize behavioral shifts over draconian measures.
    
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Shaping Rewards ($R_{\mathrm{Shaping}}$):} To overcome the sparse reward problem and guide the agent out of local optima, two specific "Heuristic Bonuses" are integrated:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Allocation Focus Bonus ($R_{\mathrm{Alloc}}$):} A progressive reward awarded when the agent allocates $>40\%$ of the quarterly budget to the identified critical barangay ($B_{\mathrm{crit}}$). This incentivizes the "Sequential Saturation" strategy.
        \item \textbf{Intensity Threshold Jackpot ($R_{\mathrm{Intense}}$):} A significant discrete bonus ($+20,000$) awarded if the Enforcement Intensity in a target barangay exceeds the critical behavioral threshold of $0.80$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

The weighting coefficients ($w_1, w_2, w_3$) are calibrated to reflect the operational reality of a 4th Class Municipality, where fiscal survival and the prevention of social backlash are as critical as environmental performance.

\subsection{The HuDRL Algorithm}

The implementation logic is formalized in the \textbf{Heuristic-Guided Action Selection \& Reward Shaping (HuDRL)} algorithm. It utilizes \textbf{Targeted Amplification} as a saliency filter: if the agent expresses an "intent" to fund a critical barangay, the environment multiplies that signal by a factor of $\alpha=100$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Heuristic-Guided Action Selection \& Reward Shaping (HuDRL)}
\label{alg:hudrl}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\Require $S_t$ (State Vector), $\mathcal{B}_{list}$ (Barangay Agents), $B_{cap}$ (Quarterly Budget)
\Ensure $A_{final}$ (Optimized Budget Allocation)

\State \textbf{Step 1: Observation}
\State $S_t \gets$ GetCurrentState()

\State \textbf{Step 2: Identify Critical Node}
\State $B_{crit} \gets \min_{b \in \mathcal{B}_{list}} (b.compliance\_rate)$ \Comment{Identify weakest performing barangay}

\State \textbf{Step 3: Initial Policy Prediction}
\State $A_{raw} \gets \pi_\theta(S_t)$ \Comment{Neural Network prediction}

\State \textbf{Step 4: Heuristic Targeted Amplification}
\If{$A_{raw}[B_{crit}] > \delta_{intent}$} \Comment{If agent shows intent to fund critical node}
    \State $A_{raw}[B_{crit}] \gets A_{raw}[B_{crit}] \times \alpha$ \Comment{Amplify signal (e.g., $\alpha=100$)}
    \State Decrease other allocations to balance
\EndIf

\State \textbf{Step 5: Budget Normalization (Softmax-style)}
\State $A_{final} \gets \mathrm{Softmax}(A_{raw}) \times B_{cap}$

\State \textbf{Step 6: Execution}
\State Apply $A_{final}$ to ABM Environment

\State \textbf{Step 7: Reward Shaping}
\State $R_{total} \gets R_{base} + R_{bonus}$ \Comment{Includes Jackpot for Saturation Strategy}

\State \textbf{Step 8: Network Update}
\State PPO.Update($S_t, A_{final}, R_{total}$)

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

First, the algorithm employs a mechanism of \textit{Targeted Amplification}. The environment functions as a ``saliency filter'' that actively monitors the state of all barangays. At each time step, it identifies the \textit{Critical Barangay} ($B_{crit}$) with the lowest compliance rate. The system then evaluates the agent's raw output ($A_{raw}$); if the agent allocates even a minimal non-zero fraction to $B_{crit}$—indicating a preliminary ``intent'' to address the problem area—the heuristic multiplies this signal by a factor of $\alpha=100$. This amplification forces the agent to explore the ``saturation'' strategy of overwhelming a single area with resources, allowing it to discover high-impact policies without requiring millions of random trials.

Second, the model imposes strict \textit{Budget Normalization}. Unlike standard continuous control tasks where outputs are often independent, municipal budgeting is a zero-sum game. The algorithm enforces a strict ``Softmax-style'' normalization across the action vector, ensuring that the sum of all allocations never exceeds the quarterly budget constraint ($Budget_Q$). This step mathematically guarantees that all generated policies remain fiscally feasible, regardless of the neural network's raw output intensity.

Finally, the learning process is reinforced through \textit{Reward Shaping}. To encourage the agent to adopt the ``Sequential Saturation'' behavior, the reward function includes a discrete ``Jackpot Bonus.'' Beyond the standard reward for aggregate compliance, the agent receives an additional $+20,000$ reward specifically for allocating more than 40\% of the quarterly budget to the \textit{Critical Barangay}. This artificial feedback signal bridges the temporal gap between the immediate action of spending money and the delayed result of behavioral change, effectively teaching the agent that focused intervention yields higher long-term returns than equitable distribution.

\section{Simulation and Analysis Framework}

This section details the experimental design for the study, outlining the complete process of operationalizing the coupled ABM-RL model to answer the core research questions. This framework was the "experimental" phase of the research, defining how the simulation was run and how the results were measured. It was divided into four key stages: (1) the protocol for initializing, calibrating, and training the model; (2) the design of the specific policy scenarios to be tested; (3) the performance metrics that were used to evaluate and compare the outcomes of each scenario; and (4) the sensitivity analysis to validate the model's robustness.

\subsection{Training and Validation}
The simulation framework was operationalized through a three-stage process: initialization, calibration, and large-scale training. 

First, the model was initialized using baseline data gathered from key-informant interviews with officials from all 7 barangays, loading in their specific population counts, local budgets, and current compliance rate estimates \citep{Villanueva2021, Yazawa2025}. This step was critical to ensure the Agent-Based Model (ABM) accurately captured the real-world heterogeneity and specific challenges facing each local unit, providing a relevant starting point for the policy simulation \citep{Camarillo2021}.

Second, the model underwent a rigorous calibration phase where the synthesized behavioral parameters—such as household sensitivity to fines and the strength of social norms—were systematically adjusted \citep{Taraghi2025, Ceschi2021}. The objective was to tune these unobservable parameters until the model's baseline behavior (when run without any new policy interventions) accurately reproduced the current-day, real-world compliance estimates provided by the LGU (e.g., the sim 10\% municipal rate) \citep{Jimenez2025}. This process of matching model outputs to observed real-world outcomes was essential for validating the ABM as a credible representation of the socio-environmental system before proceeding to policy testing \citep{Ma2023}.

Finally, once the ABM was calibrated, the Heuristic-Guided Deep Reinforcement Learning (HuDRL) agent's training began. This involved running over 12 simulated episodes, or "lifetimes" (e.g., 10,000 simulated 3-year periods), a high volume of interaction necessary to learn an optimal policy \citep{Dey2025}. By serving as the training environment, the ABM allowed the LGU agent to thoroughly explore the vast and complex 21-dimensional policy space through trial and error—a process that would have been impossible or unethical in the real world—to ultimately converge on a stable and optimal budget allocation strategy that maximized long-term compliance under fiscal constraints \citep{TianReview2024}.

\subsection{Policy Scenario Analysis}
The approach of testing distinct, constrained policy scenarios was a standard and necessary step in Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) studies embedded within Agent-Based Models (ABM), as it allowed researchers to isolate the causal impact of different instruments on system outcomes \citep{Jimenez2025, TianReview2024}. To answer the research questions, three core policy scenarios were simulated by manipulating the DRL agent's available action space, using its core levers: Information, Education, and Communication (IEC), Incentives, and Enforcement (penalties).

To systematically evaluate the efficacy of the DRL agent, the simulation was structured to compare the \textit{Original}, \textit{Modified}, and \textit{New} policy frameworks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Original Policy (Baseline):} Corresponded to the \textit{Pure Penalty Regime}, representing the current ``No Segregation, No Collection'' approach with existing budget constraints.
    \item \textbf{Modified Policies (Static Scenarios):} Corresponded to the \textit{Pure Incentive} and fixed \textit{Hybrid Regimes}, representing manual attempts to alter implementation strategies without AI optimization.
    \item \textbf{New Policy (Optimized Output):} Corresponded to the final \textit{Adaptive Strategy} learned by the agent in the Hybrid setting, where the allocation of funds across the three levers dynamically shifted in response to household behavior.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The specific experimental regimes were defined as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pure Incentive Regime:} Simulated a ``soft'' governance approach by limiting the DRL agent's action space to allocating funds only between IEC and Incentives (with no allocation for enforcement). This scenario tested the efficacy of relying solely on positive rewards, behavioral nudges, and social motivation to drive compliance \citep{LoanBalanay2023}. Research strongly supported that an incentive-based strategy could be effective in promoting pro-environmental behavior like waste separation \citep{Chen2023, MuZhang2021, Vorobeva2022}.
    
    \item \textbf{Pure Penalty Regime:} Simulated a ``hard'' governance approach by constraining the agent to allocating funds only between IEC and Enforcement. This scenario investigated the performance of a strategy heavily reliant on monitoring, fines, and institutional coercion \citep{Wang2023}. Given the challenges in enforcement highlighted in the context of the Ecological Solid Waste Management Act (R.A. 9003) in the Philippines, this scenario tested if a strictly punitive financial strategy could achieve superior compliance \citep{Dalugdog2021}.
    
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Regime:} Granted the agent access to its full action space (IEC, Incentives, and Enforcement). This scenario tasked the agent with finding the optimal, data-driven mix among all three policy levers. Literature suggested that an effective solution often required this sophisticated combination, using incentives to encourage behavior while employing penalties to deter non-compliance, demonstrating the superiority of a balanced, adaptive strategy over a single-instrument focus \citep{MuZhang2021, Zhao2022}. By comparing the compliance rates and cost-efficiency results across these three scenarios, the research could provide a powerful, data-driven recommendation for the LGU's long-term budget strategy \citep{TianReview2024}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Performance Metrics}
To evaluate the outcomes of the simulations and compare the success of each policy regime, four key performance metrics were defined, reflecting the multi-faceted nature of effective local governance.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Maximum Sustainable Compliance:} Defined as the highest population-weighted average compliance rate the LGU agent managed to achieve and maintain over the long term \citep{ApostolJamoralin2024, Torkayesh2021}. This established the agent's ability to solve the core environmental problem over a sustained period.
    
    \item \textbf{Cost-Effectiveness:} Calculated as the average percentage of compliance gained per \textpeso 100,000 spent. By integrating this financial ratio, the metric addressed the need for fiscal discipline and efficiency, ensuring the policy provided the most impact for its cost—a major practical concern for LGUs \citep{MedinaMijangos2020, WorldBank2022}.
    
    \item \textbf{Policy Equity:} Assessed by measuring the final variance in compliance rates between the 7 barangays \citep{Jimenez2025}. A lower variance indicated a more equitable and evenly distributed policy outcome, verifying that the DRL agent’s resource allocation strategy did not simply concentrate resources on easy-to-manage areas, but rather succeeded in raising compliance across all geographic units \citep{TianReview2024}.
    
    \item \textbf{Optimal Resource Allocation:} Analyzed for the comprehensive Hybrid regime as a key output. This metric detailed the final learned budget split, in both percentage (\%) and peso (\textpeso) terms, across the three policy levers (IEC, Enforcement, Incentives) and all 7 barangays. This provided the essential prescriptive, actionable intelligence, revealing the data-driven policy mix deemed optimal for the LGU's specific context \citep{MuZhang2021, Zhao2022}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Global Sensitivity Analysis}
To validate the structural integrity of the model and ensure its findings were not artifacts of specific initialization values, a Global Sensitivity Analysis (GSA) was conducted using the method called \cite{Sobol2001}.

This rigorous validation technique decomposed the variance of the model's output (Global Compliance) into fractions attributable to specific input parameters. The analysis stress-tested the model by varying the five core behavioral parameters ($w_a, w_{sn}, w_{pbc}, c_{effort}, decay$) by $\pm 20\%$.

Two indices were calculated for each parameter:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-Order Index ($S_1$):} Measured the direct contribution of the parameter to the output variance.
    \item \textbf{Total-Order Index ($S_T$):} Measured the contribution including interactions with other parameters.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethical Considerations and Limitations}
This study ensured the confidentiality of all associated entities by strictly adhering to ethical data use standards and the provisions of the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). Consistent with the Act’s mandate on the protection of personal information, no private individual or household data was collected or utilized. The model was predicated upon synthesized socio-demographic data derived exclusively from public aggregates, such as official figures from the Philippine Statistics Authority \citep{PSA2024}, and anonymized operational details obtained from LGU and barangay officials. This approach allowed the model to capture the necessary geographic and demographic complexity \citep{Jimenez2025} while fully protecting the privacy of citizens and local officials.

This research also acknowledged its inherent limitations. The computational model was an abstraction of reality \citep{Brugiere2022} and, as such, functioned only as a simplification. The findings were contingent upon synthesized behavioral parameters—like household sensitivity to fines or incentives—drawn from the extant literature \citep[e.g.,][]{Chen2023, MuZhang2021} and key-informant data, not from costly, primary household surveys. Consequently, the framework was intended strictly as a decision-support tool for exploring policy trade-offs, not as an automated-policy-making mechanism. Furthermore, a major real-world constraint was that the model could not capture the nuanced political or social feasibility of strictly enforcing penalties \citep{Nishimura2022}. This gap between algorithmic optimality and governance reality, which was specifically identified by the MENRO Head (refer to Appendix \ref{appendix:menro_interview}), confirmed that the DRL-derived optimal policy had to be carefully assessed for its implementation viability before adoption \citep{Dalugdog2021}.
