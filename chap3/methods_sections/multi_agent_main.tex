The model was developed in an existing Agent-Based Modelling Software Python library called \textit{MESA} \citep{Mesa2025}, a methodological choice strongly supported by recent reviews of computational tools in solid waste management \citep{TianReview2024, Ma2023}. Its architecture consisted of one single ABM environment that contained 7 distinct \texttt{BarangayAgent} objects, which in turn contained their respective populations of \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects.

The simulation utilized a single, discrete ABM environment to contain its operational entities and manage its temporal structure. Time progression was segmented into recurring quarterly loops, establishing the fundamental unit of analysis and adaptation for the governance agents. The architecture was strictly hierarchical: it contained seven distinct \texttt{BarangayAgent} objects in the upper layer, which encapsulated their respective populations of \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects in the lower layer. This multi-level approach was essential for accurately capturing the flow of mandates and the heterogeneity between different governance units in socio-environmental systems \citep{Brugiere2022}.

The \textit{BarangayAgents} embodied the frontline implementation layer, responsible for baseline fiscal management, policy execution (e.g., establishing waste segregation mandates), and personnel management (deployment of enforcement staff) using their own separate funds. This aligned with the decentralized governance structure where local units were the primary implementers of environmental law \citep{Nishimura2022}. The \textit{BarangayAgents} were the key aggregators for the feedback mechanism, utilizing the combined compliance rate as an observational State and prescriptive Reward signal transmitted to the central \textbf{Mayor Agent} (the Deep Reinforcement Learning mechanism) \citep{Jimenez2025}.

The \textit{EnforcementAgents} functioned as the operational execution arm, tasked with the stochastic monitoring of compliance. Constrained by the budget allocation, these agents conducted random inspections to verify waste segregation at the source, effectively operationalizing the "No Segregation, No Collection" policy \citep{Badua2022}. Their presence directly manipulated the "perceived intensity of enforcement" variable within the simulation, transforming abstract policy mandates into tangible risks for non-compliant households \citep{Carpio2025}. This agent layer introduced the critical dynamic of detection probability: while higher enforcement density significantly deterred non-compliance ("green criminology"), it incurred higher operational costs, forcing the system to balance fiscal sustainability with strict environmental policing \citep{Chen2023, Ibanez2022}.

The \textit{HouseholdAgents} represented the citizenry and were geographically and administratively segregated by their assigned barangay. Household behavior was modeled through a process of rational bounded observation, where agents assessed three primary exogenous factors: the clarity and strictness of local policies, the perceived intensity of enforcement (driven by the \textit{EnforcementAgents}), and the compliance behavior of neighboring households (social norms) \citep{Meng2018, Liao2024}. This assessment culminated in an independent segregation decision, which dictated the agent's contribution to the collective compliance level. The quarterly feedback loop aggregated these decisions into seven discrete \textit{BarangayComplianceRate} values, which were then transmitted back to the Mayor Agent, closing the adaptive system loop and facilitating the study of municipal policy response to citizen behavior \citep{Ceschi2021, Fontaine2024}.

\subsection{Formalization as a Markov Decision Process}

To facilitate the autonomous discovery of optimal strategies in the absence of longitudinal historical data, the interaction between the Mayor Agent (representing the municipal Local Government Unit) and the household agents was formalized as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP). This mathematical framework transitioned the Agent-Based Model from a descriptive simulation into a prescriptive optimization environment, where the Mayor Agent iteratively interacted with a stochastic state space to maximize a cumulative utility function \citep{Kompella2020}. The MDP is formally defined by the quintuple $\mathcal{M} = \langle S, A, P, R, \gamma \rangle$, representing the state space, action space, transition probability, reward signal, and discount factor, respectively.

The State Space ($S$) is characterized as a multi-dimensional, continuous vector $S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{17}$, which provides the Mayor Agent with a comprehensive snapshot of the municipal environment at each decision epoch. This vector encompasses the mean compliance rates across the seven constituent barangays, the normalized remaining municipal budget for the fiscal year, and the simulation time index to account for seasonal or temporal variations in waste generation. By including these macroscopic variables, the Mayor Agent can perceive the heterogeneous social landscape of the municipality, allowing for localized rather than monolithic policy responses \citep{HaMinh2025}. 

Correspondingly, the Action Space ($A$) is defined as a continuous vector $A_t \in \mathbb{R}^{21}$ belonging exclusively to the Mayor Agent. This high-dimensional space represents the granular allocation of the quarterly municipal intervention budget across the three primary policy levers—Enforcement, Incentives, and Information Education Campaigns (IEC)—for each individual barangay. Crucially, the \textit{BarangayAgents'} base budgets are executed deterministically; it is the Mayor Agent that dynamically controls the supplemental funds. The choice of a continuous action space over a discrete one is critical for achieving "policy precision," as it enables the Mayor Agent to fine-tune resource intensity to the specific tipping points of household behavioral change \citep{Rajesh2025}.

The system dynamics are governed by the Transition Function ($P$), denoted as $P(S_{t+1} | S_t, A_t)$. Within this framework, the ABM serves as the implicit transition engine; unlike classical control problems where transitions are defined by explicit differential equations, the state evolution here emerges from the aggregate, non-linear behaviors of heterogeneous household agents updating their internal utility based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). Consequently, the simulation acts as a generative model where the subsequent state $S_{t+1}$ is a stochastic manifestation of the community’s collective response to both the barangay baseline and the Mayor Agent's active policy intervention $A_t$ \citep{TianReview2024, Jimenez2025}. To evaluate the efficacy of these interventions, the Reward Function ($R$) provides a scalar feedback signal $R_t$ that balances the Mayor Agent’s multi-objective mandates. This function is designed to reward high aggregate compliance while penalizing fiscal overspending and the social friction associated with excessive punitive enforcement \citep{Jimenez2025}. Finally, a Discount Factor of $\gamma = 0.99$ is applied to the objective function, ensuring that the Mayor Agent prioritizes strategies that foster long-term, sustained behavioral shifts rather than pursuing ephemeral, short-term compliance spikes that may deplete the annual municipal budget prematurely.
