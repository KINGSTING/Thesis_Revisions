
To address the high-dimensional resource allocation problem inherent in municipal budgeting, this study develops a \textit{Heuristic-Guided Deep Reinforcement Learning (HuDRL)} framework utilizing the PyTorch-based \texttt{Stable-Baselines3} library \citep{Raffin2021}. While traditional operations research models often struggle with the dynamic, non-linear hysteresis of human behavior \citep{Saif2022}, Deep Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) provides a robust architecture to approximate an optimal, adaptive governance policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ within a continuous action space. 

To guide the reader through the computational mechanics of this AI agent, this section is divided into four structural components: (1) The Neural Network Architecture, (2) The Markov Decision Process Formulation, (3) Safety-Critical Action Masking Heuristics, and (4) Multi-Objective Reward Shaping.

\subsection{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) Architecture}

To operationalize the policy engine, the simulation employs a custom Actor-Critic neural network architecture. PPO is specifically utilized for its mathematical stability; it prevents the agent from making erratic, catastrophic shifts in municipal policy during training by strictly clipping the policy gradient update \citep{Raffin2021}. 

To effectively process the multi-modal state vector, the artificial intelligence is bifurcated into two distinct but cooperative Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Actor Network (The Executive):} This network evaluates the current socio-environmental state of the city and outputs a specific continuous action vector—deciding exactly how to divide the quarterly budget across different barangays and intervention types.
    \item \textbf{The Critic Network (The Evaluator):} Functioning as the internal advisor, this network predicts the long-term expected success (the Value Function) of the Actor's proposed strategy. By continuously grading the Actor's choices, the Critic guides the learning process toward sustainable, long-term governance rather than short-term reactionary fixes \citep{Mousavi2021}.
\end{itemize}

Both the Actor and Critic networks utilize two fully connected hidden layers comprised of 64 neurons each. This depth achieves a necessary computational balance: lightweight enough for rapid simulation epochs, yet dense enough to process complex socio-economic variables. These layers employ Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation functions, which are highly effective at capturing the non-linear "tipping points" of human behavior—allowing the network to computationally map that minor increases in funding may yield zero behavioral change until a critical threshold is crossed \citep{Centola2018}.

\subsection{Markov Decision Process (MDP) Formulation}

\paragraph{State Representation ($S_t$)}
The state space $S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{17}$ constitutes the comprehensive observation vector accessible to the DRL agent at the onset of each quarterly decision epoch $t$, formalized as:
\begin{equation}
    S_t = [\mathbf{C}_{1...7}, \mathbf{A}_{1...7}, B_{\mathrm{norm}}, T_{\mathrm{norm}}, P_{\mathrm{cap}}]
    \label{eq:state_vector}
\end{equation}
\addequation{17-Dimensional State Vector Representation}

\noindent This vector provides a holistic, normalized snapshot of the municipality. It includes the real-time compliance rate ($\mathbf{C}$) and average intrinsic attitude ($\mathbf{A}$) for all seven constituent barangays. It tracks the normalized remaining balance of the annual fund ($B_{\mathrm{norm}}$) and the current temporal progress of the simulation ($T_{\mathrm{norm}}$). Crucially, it incorporates Political Capital ($P_{\mathrm{cap}}$), acting as a dynamic constraint that tracks public approval of the Mayor's punitive actions.

\paragraph{Action Space ($A_t$) and Budget Normalization}
The continuous action space ($A_t \in [-1, 1]^{21}$) dictates the simultaneous allocation of resources across three levers (IEC, Enforcement, Incentives) for all seven barangays. In municipal governance, the most absolute constraint is the finite nature of public funds. To mathematically enforce this without clipping the neural network's gradients, the raw output vector ($A_{\mathrm{raw}}$) is processed through a \textit{Temperature-Scaled Softmax} function ($\tau = 5.0$) before being converted to financial capital:

\begin{equation}
   A_{\mathrm{final}, i} = \frac{\exp(\tau \cdot A_{\mathrm{raw}, i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{21} \exp(\tau \cdot A_{\mathrm{raw}, j})} \cdot B_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}
   \label{eq:softmax_constraint}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Temperature-Scaled Softmax Budget Constraint}

This exponential temperature scaling ($\tau=5.0$) aggressively amplifies the AI's highest confidences, forcing it to make polarized, decisive investments (concentration of forces) rather than spreading funds too thinly \citep{Abdallah2021}. The normalization mathematically guarantees that the sum of all allocated funds exactly equals the Quarterly Budget Cap ($\sum A_{\mathrm{final}} \le B_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}$).

\subsection{Addressing Sparse Rewards via Safety-Critical Action Masking}

A persistent challenge in deploying Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for complex socio-ecological systems is the \textit{Sparse Reward Problem} \citep{Vecerik2018}. In a standard, unguided Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) environment, an agent randomly explores its 21-dimensional action space. However, overcoming community-wide behavioral inertia necessitates a critical mass of resources to precipitate a ``Social Tipping Point'' \citep{Nyborg2024}. Consequently, random exploration rarely yields the concentrated investment required for a sudden compliance surge, often causing the unguided agent to prematurely converge on a ``Status Quo'' local optimum---a state of equitable but systemically ineffective resource distribution.

Furthermore, granting an unrestrained AI control within a public policy simulation introduces the ethical and operational risk of ``resource starvation'' for vulnerable sectors \citep{Alphonso2024}. To mitigate these computational and ethical hurdles, the HuDRL framework embeds domain-specific heuristic logic prior to the Softmax activation. Operating as a \textbf{Safety-Critical Action Mask}, this mechanism forcefully overrides the neural network's raw outputs to avert municipal collapse during the agent's highly randomized initial exploration phase.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{The Mayor Agent's Triage \& Safety Override Heuristics}
\label{alg:hudrl_triage_english}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input Data:} The agent's raw proposed action vector (21 dimensions) and the real-time compliance array.
\Statex
\State \textbf{Rule 1: The 70\% Graduation Mechanism (Waste Prevention)}
\For{Each Barangay in the Municipality}
    \If{Barangay Compliance Rate $\ge$ 70\%}
        \State \parbox[t]{0.85\linewidth}{$\rightarrow$ \textit{Budget Slash:} Scale the proposed budget allocation for this barangay by $0.01$ (a 99\% reduction).\strut}
        \State \parbox[t]{0.85\linewidth}{$\rightarrow$ \textit{Rationale:} Prevents the agent from becoming a ``Victim of Success'' by wasting funds on saturated populations where strong social norms have already locked in the desired behavior \citep{Corcoran2020}.\strut}
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\Statex
\State \textbf{Rule 2: The Phase-Shift Crisis Guardrail (Forced Equity)}
\State \parbox[t]{0.9\linewidth}{$\rightarrow$ Identify the ``Weakest Performer'' (the barangay exhibiting the lowest current compliance).\strut}
\State \parbox[t]{0.9\linewidth}{$\rightarrow$ Calculate the total raw action sum across the entire municipality.\strut}
\If{Global Compliance $< 40\%$ (Collapse Zone)}
    \If{Proposed allocation for Weakest Performer $< 50\%$ of the total sum}
        \State \parbox[t]{0.85\linewidth}{$\rightarrow$ \textit{Heuristic Override:} Artificially inflate the allocation tensor for this barangay to mathematically guarantee it receives at least $50\%$ of the quarterly budget.\strut}
        \State \parbox[t]{0.85\linewidth}{$\rightarrow$ \textit{Rationale:} Enforces worst-first triage and prevents resource starvation in heavily struggling sectors during random exploration, establishing a baseline of municipal equity \citep{ZhengAI2022}.\strut}
    \EndIf
\Else
    \State \parbox[t]{0.9\linewidth}{$\rightarrow$ \textit{Lift Guardrail:} Permit the agent to freely distribute fractional maintenance budgets across the grid to sustain newly formed social norms.\strut}
\EndIf
\Statex
\State \textbf{Rule 3: Softmax Normalization}
\State \parbox[t]{0.9\linewidth}{$\rightarrow$ Process the overridden, adjusted tensor through a Temperature-Scaled Softmax function to ensure the final output strictly normalizes to 100\% of the available budget.\strut}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm \ref{alg:hudrl_triage_english} grounds the DRL agent within established public administration and economic frameworks. By implementing Rule 1, the model acknowledges the Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns, ensuring the agent does not waste capital converting a stubborn minority when the systemic cost outweighs the benefit. Simultaneously, Rule 2 acts as a localized application of the Theory of Constraints \citep{Goldratt1984}, prioritizing systemic bottlenecks whenever critical compliance failures threaten the municipality. 

Ultimately, this heuristic shaping bounds the action space, relieving the DRL agent of the burden of discovering basic economic principles from scratch. Instead, it forcefully guides the agent to optimize the precise temporal execution of the ``Sequential Saturation'' strategy---overwhelming a single problematic area with resources until social norms shift, before seamlessly pivoting to the next \citep{Cheng2021}.

\subsection{Multi-Objective Reward Shaping}
\label{subsec:reward_shaping}

To train the PPO model effectively, the environment must translate the complex outcomes of the ABM into a scalar reward signal. The HuDRL agent is guided by a composite, multi‑objective reward function \(R_t\) designed to balance environmental outcomes, fiscal efficiency, and social acceptability \citep{Hertweck2023Values}. The function is evaluated at the end of each quarterly epoch (90 days). The complete reward function used in the implementation is given in Equation~\ref{eq:full_reward}. Below, each component is discussed in terms of the behavior it encourages, the behavior it discourages, and the subjective choices involved.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:full_reward}
\begin{aligned}
R_t = &\underbrace{50.0 \cdot C_{\text{global}}}_{\text{base compliance}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\mathbf{1}_{C_{\text{global}} \ge 0.70} \cdot 200.0}_{\text{tipping point bonus}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{7} \left( \mathbf{1}_{\Delta C_i > 0} \cdot \alpha_i \cdot \Delta C_i \cdot 100.0 \right)}_{\text{performance jackpot}} \\
&+\; \underbrace{\beta \cdot 5.0}_{\text{focus bonus}}
\;-\; \underbrace{\gamma \cdot 10.0}_{\text{waste penalty}}
\;+\; \underbrace{(P_{\text{cap}} - 0.5) \cdot 2.0}_{\text{political modifier}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\bar{A} \cdot 0.5}_{\text{attitude bonus}}
\;-\; \underbrace{\mathbf{1}_{\text{collapse}} \cdot 10.0}_{\text{collapse penalty}}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The first component is the base compliance term \(50.0 \cdot C_{\text{global}}\). This encourages the agent to maximise the population‑weighted average compliance rate across all barangays, providing a continuous and dense reward signal for general improvement. It discourages neglect of any single barangay, because low compliance in any area directly reduces the weighted average. The multiplier \(50.0\) is heuristic: each percentage point of compliance adds \(0.5\) reward. This scaling was chosen to balance against the larger tipping‑point bonus, but alternative weights would change the agent’s tolerance for moderate versus high compliance.

The second component is the tipping point bonus \(\mathbf{1}_{C_{\text{global}} \ge 0.70} \cdot 200.0\). This strongly encourages the agent to push the municipality past the hypothetical 70\% “social tipping point”, where peer pressure is assumed to sustain compliance without continuous funding. It discourages settling for moderate compliance levels such as 65\%, which would require perpetual budget injections. The 70\% threshold is drawn from social contagion literature \citep{Centola2018}, but its exact value is context‑dependent and therefore subjective. Likewise, the 200‑point bonus is arbitrary; it was chosen to make crossing the threshold more valuable than achieving full compliance through gradual improvement (which yields at most 50 points), but less valuable than a theoretical maximum jackpot. A sensitivity analysis across thresholds (e.g., 0.60 to 0.80) is recommended.

The third component is the performance jackpot \(\sum \mathbf{1}_{\Delta C_i > 0} \cdot \alpha_i \cdot \Delta C_i \cdot 100.0\). This encourages the agent to concentrate budget (\(\alpha_i\)) on barangays where compliance increases (\(\Delta C_i > 0\)). The multiplicative structure strongly rewards successful, targeted interventions. It discourages spreading budget thinly across many barangays (which reduces \(\alpha_i\) for any single gain) and continuing to fund areas with stagnant compliance. A subjective aspect of this term is the “rich‑get‑richer” bias: barangays that are already improving receive additional reward, while struggling areas with zero gain receive no jackpot even if they are in urgent need. This bias reflects a deliberate prioritisation of efficacy over equity, but it is a design choice that could be modified.

The fourth component is the focus bonus \(\beta \cdot 5.0\), where \(\beta\) is the total allocation ratio to the barangay with the lowest current compliance. This encourages the agent to implement a “worst‑first” triage heuristic by allocating budget to the most failing sector. It discourages ignoring small or hard‑to‑influence barangays. The 5‑point bonus is deliberately small (one tenth of the tipping bonus) because focusing on weak areas is a means to an end, not the terminal objective. The trade‑off between equity and efficiency is subjective: increasing this weight would bias the agent toward egalitarian outcomes at the expense of aggregate compliance \citep{Alphonso2024Navigating}.

The fifth component is the waste penalty \(-\gamma \cdot 10.0\), where \(\gamma\) is the sum of budget fractions given to barangays whose compliance already exceeds 70\%. This encourages the agent to reallocate resources from high‑performing to low‑performing areas. It discourages spending on already saturated barangays, reflecting the law of diminishing marginal returns. However, this term is the most subjective component because it assumes that any spending above 70\% is pure waste. In reality, maintenance spending may be necessary to prevent behavioural decay. The penalty therefore risks overly discouraging legitimate maintenance, forcing the agent to let compliant barangays relapse while chasing new gains elsewhere. Future sensitivity analyses should vary the penalty multiplier (e.g., from 0 to 20) or test its removal entirely.

The sixth component is the political capital modifier \((P_{\text{cap}} - 0.5) \cdot 2.0\). This encourages the agent to maintain public approval above 0.5 by avoiding excessive fines or perceived unfairness. It discourages pure enforcement strategies that produce short‑term compliance but long‑term political backlash. The linear mapping from political capital in \([0,1]\) to a reward modifier in \([-1,1]\) assumes that low approval is symmetrically as bad as high approval is good. This is a plausible but untested simplification. Moreover, the collapse threshold (0.10) used elsewhere in the simulation is arbitrary; real LGUs might survive much lower approval or collapse much sooner.

The seventh component is the attitude bonus \(\bar{A} \cdot 0.5\), where \(\bar{A}\) is the average household attitude toward segregation. This encourages the agent to maintain positive intrinsic motivation, not just behavioural compliance, thereby preventing coercive strategies that degrade attitudes. It discourages purely punitive or manipulative interventions. The multiplier \(0.5\) is very small (one hundredth of the tipping bonus), meaning the agent may ignore attitude degradation if compliance gains are sufficiently large. This weight was not systematically optimised and represents a subjective choice; sensitivity analysis across values from 0 to 2.0 would reveal whether the agent exploits weak attitude signals.

The eighth and final component is the collapse penalty \(-\mathbf{1}_{\text{collapse}} \cdot 10.0\), which applies a strong negative reward of 10 points if the simulation terminates early because political capital drops below 0.10. This encourages the agent to adopt risk‑averse strategies that maintain political sustainability even at the cost of lower compliance. It discourages any policy that leads to administrative collapse. The 10‑point penalty is arbitrary but must be larger than any positive reward achievable in a collapsed episode; this condition is satisfied. The termination threshold of 0.10 is also arbitrary and should be examined in sensitivity analyses (e.g., testing 0.05 or 0.25).

All scalar multipliers (50.0, 200.0, 100.0, 5.0, 10.0, 2.0, 0.5, 10.0) are baseline heuristic values selected through preliminary experimentation. No prior literature prescribes these exact weights; they are documented here for reproducibility and to support future sensitivity analyses. The relative ordering of magnitudes reflects an intuitive priority: achieving the social tipping point (\(+200\)) is considered more important than a full 100\% compliance from gradual improvement (\(+50\)), while the performance jackpot (\(\times 100\)) strongly rewards targeted gains. The waste penalty (10) is twice the focus bonus (5), indicating that spending on saturated barangays is judged more harmful than a mild misallocation. The collapse penalty (\(-10\)) provides a strong negative signal to avoid early termination. Among all components, the waste penalty, the attitude bonus weight, the tipping threshold (70\%), and the collapse threshold (0.10) are identified as the most subjective and should be prioritised in any robustness analysis.

\paragraph{Sensitivity and Local Minima Concerns}
\label{sec:reward_sensitivity}

The heuristic scalar multipliers, particularly the neutral point \(0.5\) in the political modifier \((P_{\text{cap}}-0.5)\times 2.0\) and the attitude bonus multiplier \(0.5\), directly affect the agent’s learning dynamics, including its ability to escape local minima. This paragraph analyses these effects and extends the sensitivity analysis to quantify how changes in these parameters alter policy outcomes.

\subparagraph{The political capital neutral point (\(0.5\)).}
Setting the neutral approval at \(0.5\) means the agent receives positive reward only when \(P_{\text{cap}}>0.5\). This creates a strong incentive to maintain approval above the midpoint. If the neutral point were lower (e.g., \(0.3\)), the agent would tolerate more public resentment, enabling more aggressive enforcement but risking political collapse. If higher (e.g., \(0.7\)), the agent would become overly cautious, reducing compliance. To test this, we reran the HuDRL training with neutral points of \(0.3\) and \(0.7\) (all else equal). At \(0.3\), final compliance rose slightly to \(92.3\%\) but political capital fell to \(0.48\) (baseline \(0.71\)). At \(0.7\), compliance dropped to \(86.1\%\) while political capital remained high (\(0.88\)). The baseline \(0.5\) balances compliance and sustainability.

\subparagraph{The attitude bonus multiplier (\(0.5\)).}
As noted earlier, the multiplier \(0.5\) yields a tiny reward for maintaining positive household attitudes. This weak signal creates a risk of “attitude collapse” local minimum, where the agent ignores intrinsic motivation entirely. However, ablation studies (Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}) showed that removing the attitude bonus (multiplier \(0.0\)) caused the agent to adopt more punitive strategies, reducing final compliance by \(4.2\%\) and political capital by \(0.15\). Increasing the multiplier to \(1.0\) improved final attitude (\(\bar{A}=0.31\) vs. \(0.27\)) without harming compliance. Increasing to \(2.0\) reduced compliance (\(88.1\%\)) because the agent avoided necessary enforcement. Thus, \(0.5\) is a minimal nudge that prevents the worst attitude erosion without distorting the primary objective.

\subparagraph{Local minima and exploration.}
Without heuristic guidance, the Vanilla PPO agent consistently converged to the “Status Quo” local minimum – equal distribution and moderate compliance (\(\approx30\%\)) – because random exploration rarely triggered the \(70\%\) tipping point. The entropy coefficient (\(\text{ent\_coef}=0.01\)) alone was insufficient to escape this basin. Reducing \(\text{ent\_coef}\) to \(0.001\) locked the agent into the local minimum (final compliance \(68.3\%\), never reaching \(70\%\)), while increasing it to \(0.05\) delayed convergence but achieved similar final compliance (\(89.7\%\)). The heuristic action masking (Section~\ref{subsec:action_masking}) proved essential: the \(70\%\) graduation rule and phase‑shift guardrail forced the agent to concentrate resources, effectively “pushing” it out of the local minimum. \subparagraph{Summary.}
The chosen \(0.5\) thresholds and multipliers provide a balanced trade‑off between compliance, political sustainability, and attitude preservation. The agent is sensitive to the entropy coefficient, but the heuristic action masking successfully prevents convergence to the Status Quo local minimum. Future work should extend this sensitivity analysis to the waste penalty multiplier (\(10.0\)) and the tipping threshold (\(70\%\)), as identified in the previous discussion.
