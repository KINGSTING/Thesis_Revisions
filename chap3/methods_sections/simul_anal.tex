This framework was the "experimental" phase of the research, defining how the simulation was run and how the results were measured. It was divided into four key stages: (1) the protocol for initializing, calibrating, and training the model; (2) the design of the specific policy scenarios to be tested; (3) the performance metrics that were used to evaluate and compare the outcomes of each scenario; and (4) the sensitivity analysis to validate the model's robustness.

\subsection{The Macro Simulation Driver (Temporal \& Budget Loop)}

The simulation operates on a highly structured dual timescale designed to capture both micro-level human behavior and macro-level administrative governance \citep{Brugiere2022}. At the micro-level, the environment processes daily ticks where individual agents---such as households and enforcement personnel---execute their routine interactions, evaluate their behavioral friction, and make daily segregation decisions \citep{Meng2018}. Concurrently, the macro-level operates on a 90-day (quarterly) epoch, which serves as the fundamental unit of analysis and adaptation for the governance agents \citep{Akbarpour2021}. This quarterly interval triggers the central policy evaluation, allowing the Local Government Unit (LGU) to observe the accumulated socio-behavioral shifts from the daily ticks and adapt its resource allocation strategy for the subsequent cycle to maximize long-term compliance \citep{Jimenez2025}.


\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Main Simulation Loop}
\label{alg:main_loop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\Require Annual Budget, Total Ticks (e.g., 1080 for 3 years)
\State Initialize Model Environment and Agents (Households, Enforcers, Barangays)
\Statex
\For{each tick $t$ from 1 to Total Ticks}
    \If{$t \pmod{90} == 0$}
        \State \Comment{Start of a new quarter}
        \State $Quarter \gets Quarter + 1$
        \Statex
        \State \Comment{1. Run Decision Logic (AI or Manual Policy)}
        \State $State \gets \text{Get\_Current\_Environment\_State}()$
        \State $Action\_Vector \gets \text{Calculate\_Allocation}(State)$
        \State $\text{Apply\_Budget\_Actions}(Action\_Vector)$
        \Statex
        \State \Comment{2. Reset household redemption status}
        \For{each HouseholdAgent}
            \State $Redemption\_Status \gets \text{False}$
        \EndFor
    \EndIf
    \Statex
    \State \Comment{3. Daily Updates}
    \For{each Barangay}
        \State Execute Barangay step
    \EndFor
    \For{each Agent}
        \State Execute Agent step (Household, Enforcement)
    \EndFor
    \Statex
    \State \Comment{4. System Maintenance}
    \State $\text{Update\_Political\_Capital}()$
    \State $\text{Calculate\_Daily\_Operating\_Costs}()$
    \State $\text{Collect\_Data\_Metrics}()$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The main simulation loop is driven by two primary inputs: the \textit{Annual Budget} and \textit{Total Ticks}. The \textit{Annual Budget} establishes the strict fiscal constraints of the municipality, forcing the policy engine to solve the multi-objective optimization problem without violating hard financial limits \citep{Abdallah2021}. The \textit{Total Ticks} parameter defines the finite temporal horizon of the simulation (e.g., 1080 ticks representing a 3-year period), aligning the Markov Decision Process (MDP) with the standard political term of local government officials \citep{Akbarpour2021}.

Crucially, this loop serves as the structural bridge connecting the descriptive Agent-Based Model (ABM) environment to the prescriptive Reinforcement Learning (RL) policy engine \citep{Kompella2020}. As the loop executes, the ABM acts as a high-fidelity data generator, accumulating the complex, non-linear compliance behaviors of heterogeneous household agents \citep{Tian2024}. At each 90-day epoch, these behavioral observations are aggregated into a multi-dimensional state vector and transmitted to the RL agent, which then processes this environmental feedback to calculate a newly optimized policy action—specifically, the dynamic reallocation of funds across incentives, enforcement, and educational campaigns \citep{Ha2025}.

\subsection{Training and Validation}
The simulation framework was operationalized through a three-stage process: initialization, calibration, and large-scale training. 

First, the model was initialized using baseline data gathered from key-informant interviews with officials from all 7 barangays, loading in their specific population counts, local budgets, and current compliance rate estimates \citep{Villanueva2021, Yazawa2025}. This step was critical to ensure the Agent-Based Model (ABM) accurately captured the real-world heterogeneity and specific challenges facing each local unit, providing a relevant starting point for the policy simulation \citep{Camarillo2021}.

Second, the model underwent a rigorous calibration phase where the synthesized behavioral parameters—such as household sensitivity to fines and the strength of social norms—were systematically adjusted \citep{Taraghi2025, Ceschi2021}. The objective was to tune these unobservable parameters until the model's baseline behavior (when run without any new policy interventions) accurately reproduced the current-day, real-world compliance estimates provided by the LGU (e.g., the sim 10-15\% municipal rate) \citep{Jimenez2025}. This process of matching model outputs to observed real-world outcomes was essential for validating the ABM as a credible representation of the socio-environmental system before proceeding to policy testing \citep{Ma2023}.

Finally, once the ABM was calibrated, the Heuristic-Guided Deep Reinforcement Learning (HuDRL) agent's training began. This involved running over 12 simulated episodes, or "lifetimes" (e.g., 10,000 simulated 3-year periods), a high volume of interaction necessary to learn an optimal policy \citep{Dey2025}. By serving as the training environment, the ABM allowed the LGU agent to thoroughly explore the vast and complex 21-dimensional policy space through trial and error—a process that would have been impossible or unethical in the real world—to ultimately converge on a stable and optimal budget allocation strategy that maximized long-term compliance under fiscal constraints \citep{TianReview2024}.

\subsection{Policy Scenario Analysis}
The approach of testing distinct, constrained policy scenarios is a standard and necessary step in Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) studies embedded within Agent-Based Models (ABM), as it allows researchers to isolate the causal impact of different instruments on system outcomes \citep{Jimenez2025, TianReview2024}. To answer the research questions and systematically evaluate the efficacy of the policy frameworks, four core scenarios were simulated by manipulating the available action space across three core municipal levers: Information, Education, and Communication (IEC), Incentives, and Enforcement (penalties).

The specific experimental regimes were defined as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Baseline - Status Quo (PURE IEC):} This scenario represented the default strategy for many Local Government Units (LGUs) operating under typical administrative and budgetary constraints. In this regime, the action space was restricted so that resources were entirely dedicated to Information, Education, and Communication campaigns. It relied purely on public awareness, moral suasion, and intrinsic motivation to drive waste segregation, with zero allocation for material rewards or active penalty enforcement. This served as the foundational benchmark to measure the marginal benefits of introducing more complex policy levers.
    
    \item \textbf{Pure Enforcement:} Simulated a ``hard'' governance approach by constraining the action space to prioritize monitoring, fines, and institutional coercion \citep{Wang2023}. Reflecting a strict ``No Segregation, No Collection'' mandate, this scenario tested whether a heavily punitive financial strategy could coerce superior compliance. This scenario is particularly relevant given the historical challenges and enforcement bottlenecks highlighted in the context of the Ecological Solid Waste Management Act (R.A. 9003) in the Philippines \citep{Dalugdog2021}.
    
    \item \textbf{Pure Incentives:} Simulated a ``soft'' governance approach by relying exclusively on positive reinforcement. In this scenario, funds were allocated toward rewards, behavioral nudges, and social motivation to drive compliance, with no budget directed toward punitive enforcement \citep{LoanBalanay2023}. Existing literature strongly supports that an incentive-based strategy can be highly effective in promoting pro-environmental behavior like source separation \citep{Chen2023, MuZhang2021, Vorobeva2022}.
    
    \item \textbf{Heuristic-Guided Deep Reinforcement Learning Approach:} This scenario represented the optimized, adaptive policy framework. The agent was granted access to its full action space, allowing it to dynamically allocate funds across IEC, Incentives, and Enforcement in response to shifting household behaviors. Crucially, the DRL's exploration was stabilized and accelerated using expert heuristics, grounding the agent's actions within realistic administrative bounds and preventing physically impossible resource allocations. Literature suggests that an effective solution often requires this sophisticated combination \citep{MuZhang2021, Zhao2022}. By comparing compliance rates and cost-efficiency across the static scenarios, this heuristic-guided DRL approach aimed to provide a powerful, data-driven recommendation for the optimal, long-term budget strategy \citep{TianReview2024}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Performance Metrics}
To evaluate the outcomes of the simulations and compare the success of each policy regime, four key performance metrics were defined, reflecting the multi-faceted nature of effective local governance.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Maximum Sustainable Compliance:} Defined as the highest population-weighted average compliance rate the LGU agent managed to achieve and maintain over the long term \citep{ApostolJamoralin2024, Torkayesh2021}. This established the agent's ability to solve the core environmental problem over a sustained period.
    
    \item \textbf{Cost-Effectiveness:} Calculated as the average percentage of compliance gained per \textpeso 100,000 spent. By integrating this financial ratio, the metric addressed the need for fiscal discipline and efficiency, ensuring the policy provided the most impact for its cost—a major practical concern for LGUs \citep{MedinaMijangos2020, WorldBank2022}.
    
    \item \textbf{Policy Equity:} Assessed by measuring the final variance in compliance rates between the 7 barangays \citep{Jimenez2025}. A lower variance indicated a more equitable and evenly distributed policy outcome, verifying that the DRL agent’s resource allocation strategy did not simply concentrate resources on easy-to-manage areas, but rather succeeded in raising compliance across all geographic units \citep{TianReview2024}.
    
    \item \textbf{Optimal Resource Allocation:} Analyzed for the comprehensive Hybrid regime as a key output. This metric detailed the final learned budget split, in both percentage (\%) and peso (\textpeso) terms, across the three policy levers (IEC, Enforcement, Incentives) and all 7 barangays. This provided the essential prescriptive, actionable intelligence, revealing the data-driven policy mix deemed optimal for the LGU's specific context \citep{MuZhang2021, Zhao2022}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Global Sensitivity Analysis}

To validate the structural integrity of the model and ensure its findings were not artifacts of specific initialization values, a Global Sensitivity Analysis (GSA) was conducted using the Sobol method \citep{Sobol2001}.

This rigorous validation technique decomposed the variance of the model's output (Global Compliance) into fractions attributable to specific input parameters. The analysis stress-tested the model by varying the five core behavioral parameters ($w_a, w_{sn}, w_{pbc}, c_{effort}, decay$) by $\pm 20\%$.

Two indices were calculated for each parameter:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-Order Index ($S_1$):} Measured the direct contribution of the parameter to the output variance.
    \item \textbf{Total-Order Index ($S_T$):} Measured the contribution including interactions with other parameters.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Global Sensitivity Analysis: Addressing Parameter Uncertainty}

Agent-Based Models (ABMs) are highly effective at simulating complex socio-environmental systems, but they frequently suffer from parameter uncertainty, particularly when dealing with unobservable psychological constructs and heuristic behavioral rules \citep{McCulloch2022}. To rigorously validate the structural integrity of the simulation and quantify this uncertainty, a variance-based Global Sensitivity Analysis (GSA) was conducted using the Sobol method \citep{Sobol2001}. 



Unlike local sensitivity analyses that alter one variable at a time, the Sobol method explores the entire multidimensional parameter space simultaneously. This sophisticated approach decomposes the variance of the model's primary macro-level outputs—specifically, the final Global Compliance rate and accumulated Political Capital—into fractions directly attributable to the individual Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) parameters and their complex, non-linear interactions \citep{Zheng2022a}. By doing so, it provides a quantitative measure of how heavily the LGU's policy outcomes rely on specific assumptions about human behavior.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Global Sensitivity Analysis (Sobol Method)}
\label{alg:sobol_gsa}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\Require Simulation Model, Total Samples ($N$)
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 1: Define the Parameter Space}
\State Define Problem dictionary with $Number\_of\_Variables = 5$
\State Define bounds for each parameter:
\State \quad - Weight\_Attitude ($w_a$): $[0.1, 0.6]$
\State \quad - Weight\_SocialNorm ($w_{sn}$): $[0.1, 0.6]$
\State \quad - Weight\_PBC ($w_{pbc}$): $[0.1, 0.6]$
\State \quad - Base\_Effort ($c_{effort}$): $[0.1, 0.5]$
\State \quad - Attitude\_Decay: $[0.001, 0.02]$
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 2: Generate Parameter Samples}
\State $Parameter\_Matrix \gets \text{Generate Saltelli Samples based on Problem and } N$
\State Initialize $Output\_Array\_Compliance$
\State Initialize $Output\_Array\_Capital$
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 3: Execute Simulation for Each Sample}
\For{each $Row$ in $Parameter\_Matrix$}
    \State Override Base\_Behavioral\_Profiles with $Row$ values
    \State Initialize Model with Policy\_Mode = ``status\_quo''
    \Statex
    \While{Model is Running}
        \State Execute Model Step
    \EndWhile
    \Statex
    \State \Comment{Extract Final Metrics}
    \State $Final\_Compliance \gets \text{Calculate\_Global\_Compliance}()$
    \State $Final\_Capital \gets \text{Get\_Political\_Capital}()$
    \Statex
    \State Append $Final\_Compliance$ to $Output\_Array\_Compliance$
    \State Append $Final\_Capital$ to $Output\_Array\_Capital$
\EndFor
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 4: Compute Sensitivity Indices}
\State $Sobol\_Indices\_Compliance \gets \text{Analyze Outputs using Sobol}(Problem, Output\_Array\_Compliance)$
\State $Sobol\_Indices\_Capital \gets \text{Analyze Outputs using Sobol}(Problem, Output\_Array\_Capital)$
\Statex
\State \Return First-Order ($S_1$) and Total-Order ($S_T$) indices for all variables
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



The parameter space was deliberately bounded to reflect plausible ranges of human behavioral drivers derived from existing socio-psychological literature \citep{Meng2018}. The psychological weights for Attitude ($w_a$), Subjective Norms ($w_{sn}$), and Perceived Behavioral Control ($w_{pbc}$) were uniformly bounded between $[0.1, 0.6]$. This wide yet constrained range ensures that no single psychological factor deterministically overpowers the decision-making equation without empirical justification, allowing the simulation to explore a diverse spectrum of household archetypes (e.g., highly conformist versus highly independent citizens). Additionally, operational constraints such as Base Effort ($c_{effort}$) and Attitude Decay were bounded at $[0.1, 0.5]$ and $[0.001, 0.02]$, respectively. 

By calculating the First-Order ($S_1$) and Total-Order ($S_T$) indices, we can isolate which specific parameters most heavily dictate systemic outcomes. Identifying these highly sensitive parameters is crucial; it not only highlights where future empirical data collection efforts by the municipality should be focused to reduce real-world uncertainty, but it also mathematically validates whether the emergent behavioral dynamics of the ABM align with established psychological theories of waste segregation \citep{Tian2024}.
