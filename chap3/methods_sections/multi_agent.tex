The model was developed in an existing Agent-Based Modelling Software Python library called \textit{MESA} \citep{Mesa2025}, a methodological choice strongly supported by recent reviews of computational tools in solid waste management \citep{TianReview2024, Ma2023}. Its architecture consisted of one single ABM environment that contained 7 distinct \texttt{BarangayAgent} objects, which in turn contained their respective populations of \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects.

The simulation utilized a single, discrete ABM environment to contain its operational entities and manage its temporal structure. Time progression was segmented into recurring quarterly loops, establishing the fundamental unit of analysis and adaptation for the governance agents. The architecture was strictly hierarchical: it contained seven distinct \texttt{BarangayAgent} objects in the upper layer, which encapsulated their respective populations of \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects in the lower layer. This multi-level approach was essential for accurately capturing the flow of mandates and the heterogeneity between different governance units in socio-environmental systems \citep{Brugiere2022}.

The \textit{BarangayAgents} embodied the Local Government Unit (LGU) implementation layer, responsible for fiscal management (fund allocation), policy formulation and implementation (e.g., establishing waste segregation mandates), and personnel management (deployment of enforcement staff). This aligned with the decentralized governance structure where local units were the primary implementers of environmental law \citep{Nishimura2022}. The \textit{BarangayAgents} were the key recipients of the feedback mechanism, utilizing the aggregated compliance rate as an observational State and a prescriptive Reward signal for its internal Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) mechanism \citep{Jimenez2025}.

The \textit{EnforcementAgents} functioned as the operational execution arm of the LGU, tasked with the stochastic monitoring of compliance. Constrained by the budget allocation determined by the \textit{BarangayAgents}, these agents conducted random inspections to verify waste segregation at the source, effectively operationalizing the "No Segregation, No Collection" policy \citep{Badua2022}. Their presence directly manipulated the "perceived intensity of enforcement" variable within the simulation, transforming abstract policy mandates into tangible risks for non-compliant households \citep{Carpio2025}. This agent layer introduced the critical dynamic of detection probability: while higher enforcement density significantly deterred non-compliance ("green criminology"), it incurred higher operational costs, forcing the system to balance fiscal sustainability with strict environmental policing \citep{Chen2023, Ibanez2022}.

The \textit{HouseholdAgents} represented the citizenry and were geographically and administratively segregated by their assigned barangay. Household behavior was modeled through a process of rational bounded observation, where agents assessed three primary exogenous factors: the clarity and strictness of LGU policies, the perceived intensity of local enforcement (driven by the \textit{EnforcementAgents}), and the compliance behavior of neighboring households (social norms) \citep{Meng2018, Liao2024}. This assessment culminated in an independent segregation decision, which dictated the agent's contribution to the collective compliance level. The quarterly feedback loop aggregated these decisions into seven discrete \textit{BarangayComplianceRate} values, which were then transmitted back to the LGU-DRL Agent, closing the adaptive system loop and facilitating the study of policy response to citizen behavior \citep{Ceschi2021, Fontaine2024}.

\subsection{Formalization as a Markov Decision Process}

To facilitate the autonomous discovery of optimal strategies in the absence of longitudinal historical data, the interaction between the Local Government Unit (LGU) and the household agents was formalized as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP). This mathematical framework transitioned the Agent-Based Model from a descriptive simulation into a prescriptive optimization environment, where the LGU-DRL agent iteratively interacted with a stochastic state space to maximize a cumulative utility function \citep{Kompella2020}. The MDP is formally defined by the quintuple $\mathcal{M} = \langle S, A, P, R, \gamma \rangle$, representing the state space, action space, transition probability, reward signal, and discount factor, respectively.

The State Space ($S$) is characterized as a multi-dimensional, continuous vector $S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$, which provides the agent with a comprehensive snapshot of the municipal environment at each decision epoch. This vector encompasses the mean compliance rates across the seven constituent barangays, the normalized remaining budget for the fiscal year, and the simulation time index to account for seasonal or temporal variations in waste generation. By including these macroscopic variables, the agent can perceive the heterogeneous social landscape of the municipality, allowing for localized rather than monolithic policy responses \citep{HaMinh2025}. Correspondingly, the Action Space ($A$) is defined as a continuous vector $A_t \in \mathbb{R}^{21}$. This high-dimensional space represents the granular allocation of the quarterly budget across the three primary policy levers—Enforcement, Incentives, and Information Education Campaigns (IEC)—for each individual barangay. The choice of a continuous action space over a discrete one is critical for achieving "policy precision," as it enables the Reinforcement Learning agent to fine-tune resource intensity to the specific tipping points of household behavioral change \citep{Rajesh2025}.

The system dynamics are governed by the Transition Function ($P$), denoted as $P(S_{t+1} | S_t, A_t)$. Within this framework, the ABM serves as the implicit transition engine; unlike classical control problems where transitions are defined by explicit differential equations, the state evolution here emerges from the aggregate, non-linear behaviors of heterogeneous household agents updating their internal utility based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). Consequently, the simulation acts as a generative model where the subsequent state $S_{t+1}$ is a stochastic manifestation of the community’s collective response to the LGU's policy intervention $A_t$ \citep{TianReview2024, Jimenez2025}. To evaluate the efficacy of these interventions, the Reward Function ($R$) provides a scalar feedback signal $R_t$ that balances the LGU’s multi-objective mandates. This function is designed to reward high aggregate compliance while penalizing fiscal overspending and the social friction associated with excessive punitive enforcement \citep{Dey2025}. Finally, a Discount Factor of $\gamma = 0.99$ is applied to the objective function, ensuring that the DRL agent prioritizes strategies that foster long-term, sustained behavioral shifts rather than pursuing ephemeral, short-term compliance spikes that may deplete the annual budget prematurely.

\subsection{Barangay Agent Design and Resource Distribution}

The \texttt{BarangayAgent} serves as the critical intermediate implementation layer, bridging the gap between municipal-level resource allocation and household-level behavioral shifts. The model initializes seven unique instances of this agent class—representing the geographically and socioeconomically distinct units of Liangan East, Esperanza, Poblacion, Binuni, Demologan, Mati, and Babalaya—calibrated with parameters derived from municipal institutional audits. Within this framework, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} functions as an executive entity that manages the ``stacking effect'' of fiscal resources, combining LGU-DRL quarterly augmentation funds with its own localized operating budget. 

When the LGU-DRL agent allocates IEC funds ($A_{\mathrm{IEC}, B_i}$) to a specific barangay, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} translates this continuous financial variable into a localized \textit{Information Dissemination Signal}. IEC campaigns are broadcast globally to all constituent \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects within that barangay, proportionally shifting the internal Attitude weight ($w_A(t)$) positively to counteract the natural stochastic decay of pro-environmental habits.

For the Incentive budget ($A_{\mathrm{Inc}, B_i}$), the \texttt{BarangayAgent} functions as a direct financial distributor. During the quarterly evaluation phase, the agent identifies the subset of households actively practicing segregation (\texttt{is\_segregating == True}) and distributes the allocated reward pool among them, which directly modifies the perceived monetary benefit ($I$) in the household's net disutility calculation. Crucially, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} manages the per-capita dilution of these funds. If the local compliance rate surges, the fixed quarterly allocation is divided among a larger pool of households, reducing individual reward magnitude. This mechanism accurately simulates the ``victim of success'' dynamic, forcing the DRL agent to continuously adjust funding to maintain motivation. At the conclusion of each quarter, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} aggregates compliance data and reports the \textit{BarangayComplianceRate} to the LGU agent, completing a high-fidelity feedback loop where local citizen behavior informs subsequent municipal-level policy adjustments.

\subsection{Enforcement Agent Design and Spatial Dynamics}

Operating under the directive of the \texttt{BarangayAgent} is the \texttt{EnforcementAgent}, which serves as the operational extension of the municipal government tasked with physically monitoring household compliance and executing punitive measures. To operationalize the ``No Segregation, No Collection'' mandate, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} deploys these local enforcers. 

To ensure ecological validity, each enforcement agent is initialized with a \texttt{base\_daily\_capacity} of 30 households. This cap reflects the geographical realities and administrative friction of monitoring the municipality. Operating on a quarterly time step of 66 standard working days, the maximum theoretical inspection capacity ($C_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}$) of an active enforcer is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{Quarterly}} = (C_{\mathrm{BaseDaily}} \times \mu_{\mathrm{eff}}) \times 66
    \label{eq:enforcement_capacity}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Theoretical Quarterly Enforcement Capacity}

\noindent where $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}$ represents an efficiency multiplier (defaulted to 1.0) that can be adjusted to simulate varying levels of institutional competence.

The physical environment of the Agent-Based Model (ABM) is constructed as a two-dimensional spatial grid, where Household and Enforcement Agents are geographically distributed to represent the topographical layout of the municipality. Unlike centralized, equation-based models that assume uniform mixing or omniscient actors, this spatial formulation enforces strict geographical limitations on agent interactions \citep{Meng2018}. Specifically, Enforcement Agents do not possess global, omniscient vision of the entire barangay. Instead, they operate under bounded rationality and physical constraints, forcing them to rely on localized spatial searches within a defined line-of-sight (e.g., $Radius = 10$). This architectural design accurately simulates the limited monitoring capacity of real-world local enforcers who can only act upon non-compliant behaviors physically observed within their immediate vicinity \citep{Dalugdog2021, TianReview2024}.

The algorithmic movement of the Enforcement Agents is bifurcated into two distinct behavioral states: ``Chase Mode'' and ``Patrol Mode.'' When in Patrol Mode, the agent conducts a stochastic spatial search, moving to random adjacent coordinates. This represents the routine, unguided neighborhood rounds conducted by personnel when no immediate waste violations are visible. However, if a non-compliant Household Agent is detected within the enforcer's scanning radius, the agent dynamically switches to Chase Mode, calculating a directed spatial trajectory toward the closest violator to execute an intervention \citep{Zhao2022}. 

During each simulation step, the deployed agent employs this randomized monitoring strategy, selecting a discrete sample of households up to its capacity limit. If a household is non-compliant, the agent flags it and increments its internal \texttt{citations\_issued} tracker, directly triggering the financial penalty ($F$) within the household's utility function. 


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Enforcement Agent Patrol \& Fining}
\label{alg:enforcement_patrol}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\Require Agent Position, Local Radius = 10
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 1: Mark Immediate Surroundings}
\State Mark all Household Agents in Radius 1 as ``Visited''
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 2: Determine Movement (Separate Patrol Logic)}
\State $Local\_Targets \gets \text{Scan for Unvisited Household Agents in Radius 10}$
\State $\text{Filter } Local\_Targets \text{ for Non-Compliant behavior}$
\Statex
\If{$Local\_Targets \text{ is NOT empty}$}
    \State \Comment{CHASE MODE}
    \State $Target \gets \text{Find Closest Agent in } Local\_Targets$
    \State $Next\_Position \gets \text{Calculate coordinate step closer to } Target$
\Else
    \State \Comment{PATROL MODE}
    \State $Next\_Position \gets \text{Select Random Valid Adjacent Coordinate}$
\EndIf
\Statex
\State $\text{Move Agent to } Next\_Position$
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 3: Enforcement Execution}
\State $Catch\_Zone \gets \text{Scan for Household Agents in Radius 1 (Include Center)}$
\For{each $Household$ in $Catch\_Zone$}
    \If{$Household \text{ is NOT Compliant}$}
        \State $\text{Apply Fine (Decrease Household Utility and Attitude)}$
        \State $\text{Increment Global Fines Collected}$
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


Crucially, the presence of these agents generates a systemic feedback loop. Following inspections, the enforcement agent updates the barangay's macro-level Perceived Probability of Detection ($P_{\mathrm{Detection}}$) using the ratio of total inspection capacity to the total household population:

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{Detection}} = \min\left(1.0, \frac{\sum C_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}}{N_{\mathrm{Households}}}\right)
    \label{eq:prob_detection}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Macro-level Perceived Probability of Detection}

This updated probability is broadcasted back to the \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects, dynamically increasing their anticipated risk. This spatial limitation profoundly reflects the real-world logistical constraints of municipal governance, particularly the deployment of \textit{barangay tanods} (village watchmen) or local environmental enforcers in the Philippines. Local Government Units (LGUs) rarely have the manpower or budgetary resources to monitor every household simultaneously \citep{Abordo2025}. Consequently, the probability of a household being caught and fined is not absolute; rather, it is a highly localized phenomenon dependent on spatial proximity and the enforcer's current patrol route. This localized enforcement mechanism organically generates the dynamic, fluctuating detection probability experienced by households, ensuring that the deterrent effect of punitive policies is realistically constrained by the physical and temporal capacity of the barangay's law enforcement \citep{Jimenez2025}.

\subsection{Household Agent Design: The TPB Framework and Utility Architecture}

The decision-making architecture of the \texttt{HouseholdAgent} is governed by a dynamic utility function grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which posits that an individual's intention to perform a behavior is a function of their attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control \citep{Ceschi2021}. In contrast to static behavioral models, this framework incorporates time-variant weights ($w(t)$) for psychological constructs, allowing agent behavior to evolve non-linearly in response to LGU interventions and social feedback loops \citep{Taraghi2025, Ma2023}. The core decision logic is represented by the utility of segregation ($U_{\mathrm{segregate}}$):

\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{segregate}} = (w_A(t) A) + (w_{SN}(t) SN_{\mathrm{local}}) + (w_{PBC}(t)  PBC) - C_{\mathrm{Net}} + \epsilon
    \label{eq:utility_function}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Household Utility of Segregation Function}

In this formulation, $w_A(t)$ represents the temporal evolution of the agent's internal valuation of segregation, functioning as a decay model that increases in response to Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) investment and decays stochastically in the absence of reinforcement to simulate ``public forgetting'' \citep{Trushna2024}. The social component, $SN_{\mathrm{local}}$, is an endogenous variable derived from the observed compliance rate of the agent's immediate spatial neighborhood, capturing the effects of social pressure and observational learning \citep{Meng2018, Liao2024}. To account for inherent uncertainty in human decision-making, $\epsilon$ introduces stochastic noise ($\mu=0, \sigma=0.05$), ensuring the model reflects real-world behavioral variance \citep{Subedi2025}.

A critical innovation in this model is the calculation of $C_{\mathrm{Net}}$, the net behavioral cost, which characterizes the perceived friction of compliance:

\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{Net}} = C_{\mathrm{Effort}} + (\gamma C_{\mathrm{Monetary}}) - (\gamma I) - (\gamma F P_{\mathrm{Detection}})
    \label{eq:net_disutility}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Net Disutility of Segregation Function}

\noindent This cost-benefit sub-model integrates both physical and financial barriers, defined as follows:

\begin{description}
    \item[$\bm{C_{\mathrm{Effort}}}$:] The physical hassle associated with washing, sorting, and storage of waste.
    \item[$\bm{C_{\mathrm{Monetary}}}$:] Tangible financial expenses, such as procuring color-coded bins or sacks.
    \item[$\bm{\gamma}$ (Income Sensitivity):] A weighting factor derived from household income level; for lower-income households, $\gamma > 1$ as financial levers ($I$ and $F$) carry greater psychological weight, whereas $\gamma < 1$ for higher-income households.
    \item[$\bm{I}$ and $\bm{F}$:] The objective magnitudes of monetary incentives and punitive fines, respectively.
    \item[$\bm{P_{\mathrm{Detection}}}$:] The likelihood of enforcement detection, dynamically linked to the LGU's resource allocation.
\end{description}

The model also captures complex psychological feedbacks such as ``psychological reactance.'' While LGU investments generally improve Attitude ($A$) and Subjective Norms ($SN$) \citep{Trushna2024}, if enforcement intensity crosses a specific threshold, Attitude may paradoxically decrease. This reflects the tendency of individuals to resist coercive mandates when they perceive a loss of autonomy \citep{LoanBalanay2023}. Finally, Perceived Behavioral Control ($PBC$) is calibrated by the agent's internal psychological friction, acknowledging that agents with strong motivation may still fail to comply if the behavior is highly disruptive to established routines.

\subsubsection{Behavioral Hysteresis: Social Shields and Norm Internalization}

Standard Agent-Based Models often suffer from hyper-reactivity, exhibiting unrealistic behavioral decay where compliance collapses immediately upon the cessation of external stimuli. To more accurately simulate the real-world stability of established habits, this study incorporates a ``Two-Stage Social Shield'' and \textit{Norm Internalization Mechanism}. This mathematically models human behavioral inertia—or ``Cultural Inertia''—where a behavior transitions from a calculated, incentive-driven decision to an internalized social habit once critical thresholds are reached \citep{Ceschi2021, Corcoran2020}.

To achieve this, the standard attitude update function is refined by a dynamic damping factor ($D_{\mathrm{factor}}$), suggesting that established social norms act as a buffer against the rapid erosion of compliance \citep{Andre2021}:

\begin{equation}
    A_{t+1} = A_t - (\delta \times D_{\mathrm{factor}})
\end{equation}
\addequation{Attitude Decay and Damping Function}

The damping factor is governed by the strength of local social norms ($SN$) through two distinct stages:

\textbf{Stage 1: Bayanihan Activation (The 40\% Floor):} Triggered when barangay compliance surpasses 40\%, representing an emergent community spirit that slows the erosion of positive norms. This acts as a \textit{Social Norm Floor}, ensuring that once a baseline awareness is achieved, perceived social pressure does not drop to zero, representing residual collective memory \citep{Nishimura2022}.

\textbf{Stage 2: Norm Lock-in (The 70\% Tipping Point):} Occurs when compliance exceeds 70\%, representing deep cultural habituation where segregation becomes the default state \citep{Jimenez2025}. This reflects ``critical mass'' theory, where minority behaviors cascade into majority conventions \citep{Centola2018, Nyborg2024}. 

Mathematically, this damping effect is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{factor}} = 
    \begin{cases} 
        0.1 & \text{if } SN > 0.70 \text{ (Stage 2: Strong Norms / Internalized)} \\
        0.5 & \text{if } SN > 0.40 \text{ (Stage 1: Bayanihan Activation)} \\
        1.0 & \text{otherwise (Weak Norms / Standard Decay)}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Dynamic Damping Factor for Behavioral Inertia}

This refinement is critical for addressing sustainability objectives, allowing the Deep Reinforcement Learning agent to discover strategies that foster self-sustaining compliance rather than perpetual reliance on high-cost incentives.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic Implementation of Household Decisions}

The integration of the dynamic utility function and the protective social shields is executed daily by each \texttt{HouseholdAgent} via Algorithm \ref{alg:household_decision}. 

If the calculated $Utility$ exceeds $0.0$, the agent's baseline intent is to segregate. However, intentionality does not guarantee perfect execution \citep{McCulloch2022}. To model this, the algorithm implements a strict ``1\% Human Error'' rule. Even when a household has a highly positive utility and full intention to comply, there is a 1\% probability that they fail to segregate on any given day due to unpredictable real-world friction (e.g., misplacing items, lack of time, or full bins). This constraint prevents the model from plateauing at an unrealistic, deterministic 100\% compliance rate, ensuring ecological validity aligned with actual municipal waste management data \citep{TianReview2024}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Household Decision-Making (TPB with Shields)}
\label{alg:household_decision}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\Require Household properties (Income, Base Attitude, Base SN, Base PBC)
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 1: Update Social Norms (SN) with Perception Cap}
\State $Local\_Compliance \gets \text{Calculate\_Neighbors\_Compliance}(Radius = 2)$
\State $Authority\_Boost \gets Enforcement\_Intensity \times 0.50$
\State $Perceived\_Reality \gets \min(0.92, Local\_Compliance + Authority\_Boost)$
\Statex
\If{$Perceived\_Reality < Current\_SN$}
    \State \Comment{Apply Damper (Inertia/Stickiness)}
    \If{$Barangay\_Compliance > 0.70$}
        \State $Current\_SN \gets (0.90 \times Current\_SN) + (0.10 \times Perceived\_Reality)$ \Comment{Stage 2}
    \ElsIf{$Barangay\_Compliance > 0.40$}
        \State $Current\_SN \gets (0.85 \times Current\_SN) + (0.15 \times Perceived\_Reality)$ \Comment{Stage 1}
    \Else
        \State $Current\_SN \gets Perceived\_Reality$
    \EndIf
\Else
    \State $Current\_SN \gets Perceived\_Reality$
\EndIf
\State $Current\_SN \gets \min(0.95, Current\_SN)$ \Comment{Realism Cap}
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 2: Update Attitude with Decay and Synergy}
\State $Decay\_Damper \gets \text{Calculate\_Damper\_Based\_On\_Compliance}()$
\State $Attitude \gets Attitude - (Decay\_Rate \times Decay\_Damper)$
\State $Attitude \gets Attitude + \text{Calculate\_IEC\_Synergy\_Boost}()$
\State $Attitude \gets \text{Apply\_Pride\_And\_Complacency\_Modifiers}(Attitude)$
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 3: Calculate Utility and Decision}
\State $Monetary\_Impact \gets Incentive\_Value + (Fine\_Amount \times Prob\_Detection)$
\State $Net\_Cost \gets Base\_Effort - (Gamma\_Income \times (Monetary\_Impact / 2000.0))$
\State $Epsilon \gets \text{Random\_Gaussian}(\text{Mean} = 0, \text{StdDev} = 0.05)$
\Statex
\If{$Utility > 0.0$}
    \State $Is\_Compliant \gets \text{True}$
    \State \Comment{Human Error Rule}
    \If{$\text{Random}(0.0, 1.0) < 0.01$}
        \State $Is\_Compliant \gets \text{False}$
    \EndIf
\Else
    \State $Is\_Compliant \gets \text{False}$
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Global Sensitivity Analysis: Addressing Parameter Uncertainty}
Agent-Based Models (ABMs) are highly effective at simulating complex socio-environmental systems, but they frequently suffer from parameter uncertainty, particularly when dealing with unobservable psychological constructs and heuristic behavioral rules \citep{McCulloch2022}. To rigorously validate the structural integrity of the simulation and quantify this uncertainty, a variance-based Global Sensitivity Analysis (GSA) was conducted using the Sobol method \citep{Sobol2001}. 



Unlike local sensitivity analyses that alter one variable at a time, the Sobol method explores the entire multidimensional parameter space simultaneously. This sophisticated approach decomposes the variance of the model's primary macro-level outputs—specifically, the final Global Compliance rate and accumulated Political Capital—into fractions directly attributable to the individual Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) parameters and their complex, non-linear interactions \citep{ZhengAI2022}. By doing so, it provides a quantitative measure of how heavily the LGU's policy outcomes rely on specific assumptions about human behavior.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Global Sensitivity Analysis (Sobol Method)}
\label{alg:sobol_gsa}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\Require Simulation Model, Total Samples ($N$)
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 1: Define the Parameter Space}
\State Define Problem dictionary with $Number\_of\_Variables = 5$
\State Define bounds for each parameter:
\State \quad - Weight\_Attitude ($w_a$): $[0.1, 0.6]$
\State \quad - Weight\_SocialNorm ($w_{sn}$): $[0.1, 0.6]$
\State \quad - Weight\_PBC ($w_{pbc}$): $[0.1, 0.6]$
\State \quad - Base\_Effort ($c_{effort}$): $[0.1, 0.5]$
\State \quad - Attitude\_Decay: $[0.001, 0.02]$
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 2: Generate Parameter Samples}
\State $Parameter\_Matrix \gets \text{Generate Saltelli Samples based on Problem and } N$
\State Initialize $Output\_Array\_Compliance$
\State Initialize $Output\_Array\_Capital$
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 3: Execute Simulation for Each Sample}
\For{each $Row$ in $Parameter\_Matrix$}
    \State Override Base\_Behavioral\_Profiles with $Row$ values
    \State Initialize Model with Policy\_Mode = ``status\_quo''
    \Statex
    \While{Model is Running}
        \State Execute Model Step
    \EndWhile
    \Statex
    \State \Comment{Extract Final Metrics}
    \State $Final\_Compliance \gets \text{Calculate\_Global\_Compliance}()$
    \State $Final\_Capital \gets \text{Get\_Political\_Capital}()$
    \Statex
    \State Append $Final\_Compliance$ to $Output\_Array\_Compliance$
    \State Append $Final\_Capital$ to $Output\_Array\_Capital$
\EndFor
\Statex
\State \Comment{Step 4: Compute Sensitivity Indices}
\State $Sobol\_Indices\_Compliance \gets \text{Analyze Outputs using Sobol}(Problem, Output\_Array\_Compliance)$
\State $Sobol\_Indices\_Capital \gets \text{Analyze Outputs using Sobol}(Problem, Output\_Array\_Capital)$
\Statex
\State \Return First-Order ($S_1$) and Total-Order ($S_T$) indices for all variables
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The parameter space was deliberately bounded to reflect plausible ranges of human behavioral drivers derived from existing socio-psychological literature \citep{Meng2018}. The psychological weights for Attitude ($w_a$), Subjective Norms ($w_{sn}$), and Perceived Behavioral Control ($w_{pbc}$) were uniformly bounded between $[0.1, 0.6]$. This wide yet constrained range ensures that no single psychological factor deterministically overpowers the decision-making equation without empirical justification, allowing the simulation to explore a diverse spectrum of household archetypes (e.g., highly conformist versus highly independent citizens). Additionally, operational constraints such as Base Effort ($c_{effort}$) and Attitude Decay were bounded at $[0.1, 0.5]$ and $[0.001, 0.02]$, respectively. 

By calculating the First-Order ($S_1$) and Total-Order ($S_T$) indices, we can isolate which specific parameters most heavily dictate systemic outcomes. Identifying these highly sensitive parameters is crucial; it not only highlights where future empirical data collection efforts by the municipality should be focused to reduce real-world uncertainty, but it also mathematically validates whether the emergent behavioral dynamics of the ABM align with established psychological theories of waste segregation \citep{TianReview2024}.
