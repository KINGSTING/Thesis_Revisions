Its architecture consisted of one single ABM environment that contained 7 distinct \texttt{BarangayAgent} objects, which in turn contained their respective populations of \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects.

The simulation utilized a single, discrete ABM environment to contain its operational entities and manage its temporal structure. Time progression was segmented into recurring quarterly loops, establishing the fundamental unit of analysis and adaptation for the governance agents. The architecture was strictly hierarchical: it contained seven distinct \texttt{BarangayAgent} objects in the upper layer, which encapsulated their respective populations of \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects in the lower layer. This multi-level approach was essential for accurately capturing the flow of mandates and the heterogeneity between different governance units in socio-environmental systems \citep{Brugiere2022}.

The \textit{EnforcementAgents} functioned as the operational execution arm, tasked with the stochastic monitoring of compliance. Constrained by the budget allocation, these agents conducted random inspections to verify waste segregation at the source, effectively operationalizing the "No Segregation, No Collection" policy \citep{Badua2022}. Their presence directly manipulated the "perceived intensity of enforcement" variable within the simulation, transforming abstract policy mandates into tangible risks for non-compliant households \citep{Carpio2025}. This agent layer introduced the critical dynamic of detection probability: while higher enforcement density significantly deterred non-compliance ("green criminology"), it incurred higher operational costs, forcing the system to balance fiscal sustainability with strict environmental policing \citep{Chen2023, Ibanez2022}.

The \textit{HouseholdAgents} represented the citizenry and were geographically and administratively segregated by their assigned barangay. Household behavior was modeled through a process of rational bounded observation, where agents assessed three primary exogenous factors: the clarity and strictness of local policies, the perceived intensity of enforcement (driven by the \textit{EnforcementAgents}), and the compliance behavior of neighboring households (social norms) \citep{Meng2018, Liao2024}. This assessment culminated in an independent segregation decision, which dictated the agent's contribution to the collective compliance level. The quarterly feedback loop aggregated these decisions into seven discrete \textit{BarangayComplianceRate} values, which were then transmitted back to the Mayor Agent, closing the adaptive system loop and facilitating the study of municipal policy response to citizen behavior \citep{Ceschi2021, Fontaine2024}.

\subsection{Formalization as a Markov Decision Process}

To facilitate the autonomous discovery of optimal strategies in the absence of longitudinal historical data, the interaction between the Mayor Agent (representing the municipal Local Government Unit) and the household agents was formalized as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP). This mathematical framework transitioned the Agent-Based Model from a descriptive simulation into a prescriptive optimization environment, where the Mayor Agent iteratively interacted with a stochastic state space to maximize a cumulative utility function \citep{Kompella2020}. The MDP is formally defined by the quintuple $\mathcal{M} = \langle S, A, P, R, \gamma \rangle$, representing the state space, action space, transition probability, reward signal, and discount factor, respectively.

The State Space ($S$) is characterized as a multi-dimensional, continuous vector $S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$, which provides the Mayor Agent with a comprehensive snapshot of the municipal environment at each decision epoch. This vector encompasses the mean compliance rates across the seven constituent barangays, the normalized remaining municipal budget for the fiscal year, and the simulation time index to account for seasonal or temporal variations in waste generation. By including these macroscopic variables, the Mayor Agent can perceive the heterogeneous social landscape of the municipality, allowing for localized rather than monolithic policy responses \citep{HaMinh2025}. 

Correspondingly, the Action Space ($A$) is defined as a continuous vector $A_t \in \mathbb{R}^{21}$ belonging exclusively to the Mayor Agent. This high-dimensional space represents the granular allocation of the quarterly municipal intervention budget across the three primary policy levers—Enforcement, Incentives, and Information Education Campaigns (IEC)—for each individual barangay. Crucially, the \textit{BarangayAgents'} base budgets are executed deterministically; it is the Mayor Agent that dynamically controls the supplemental funds. The choice of a continuous action space over a discrete one is critical for achieving "policy precision," as it enables the Mayor Agent to fine-tune resource intensity to the specific tipping points of household behavioral change \citep{Rajesh2025}.

The system dynamics are governed by the Transition Function ($P$), denoted as $P(S_{t+1} | S_t, A_t)$. Within this framework, the ABM serves as the implicit transition engine; unlike classical control problems where transitions are defined by explicit differential equations, the state evolution here emerges from the aggregate, non-linear behaviors of heterogeneous household agents updating their internal utility based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). Consequently, the simulation acts as a generative model where the subsequent state $S_{t+1}$ is a stochastic manifestation of the community’s collective response to both the barangay baseline and the Mayor Agent's active policy intervention $A_t$ \citep{TianReview2024, Jimenez2025}. To evaluate the efficacy of these interventions, the Reward Function ($R$) provides a scalar feedback signal $R_t$ that balances the Mayor Agent’s multi-objective mandates. This function is designed to reward high aggregate compliance while penalizing fiscal overspending and the social friction associated with excessive punitive enforcement \citep{Dey2025}. Finally, a Discount Factor of $\gamma = 0.99$ is applied to the objective function, ensuring that the Mayor Agent prioritizes strategies that foster long-term, sustained behavioral shifts rather than pursuing ephemeral, short-term compliance spikes that may deplete the annual municipal budget prematurely.

\subsection{Barangay Agent Design and Resource Distribution}

The \texttt{BarangayAgent} serves as the critical intermediate implementation layer, bridging the gap between municipal-level resource allocation and household-level behavioral shifts. The model initializes seven unique instances of this agent class—representing the geographically and socioeconomically distinct units of Liangan East, Esperanza, Poblacion, Binuni, Demologan, Mati, and Babalaya—calibrated with parameters derived from municipal institutional audits. 

Within this framework, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} functions as a localized environment where two distinct layers of policy operate concurrently. Rather than pooling funds, the Barangay Agent executes its baseline policy statically using its own localized operating budget. Simultaneously, it manages the ``stacking effect'' on household psychology when the central Mayor Agent injects targeted augmentation funds into the environment.

When the Mayor Agent allocates municipal IEC funds ($A_{\mathrm{IEC}, B_i}$) to a specific barangay, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} translates this continuous financial variable into a localized \textit{Information Dissemination Signal}. IEC campaigns are broadcast globally to all constituent \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects within that barangay, proportionally shifting the internal Attitude weight ($w_A(t)$) positively to counteract the natural stochastic decay of pro-environmental habits.

For the Incentive budget ($A_{\mathrm{Inc}, B_i}$), the \texttt{BarangayAgent} functions as a direct financial distributor of the Mayor Agent's funds. During the quarterly evaluation phase, the agent identifies the subset of households actively practicing segregation (\texttt{is\_segregating == True}) and distributes the allocated reward pool among them, which directly modifies the perceived monetary benefit ($I$) in the household's net disutility calculation. Crucially, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} manages the per-capita dilution of these funds. If the local compliance rate surges, the fixed quarterly municipal allocation is divided among a larger pool of households, reducing individual reward magnitude. This mechanism accurately simulates the ``victim of success'' dynamic, forcing the Mayor Agent to continuously adjust funding to maintain motivation. At the conclusion of each quarter, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} aggregates compliance data and reports the \textit{BarangayComplianceRate} to the Mayor Agent, completing a high-fidelity feedback loop where local citizen behavior informs subsequent municipal-level policy adjustments.

\subsection{Enforcement Agent Design and Spatial Dynamics}

Operating under the directive of the \texttt{BarangayAgent} is the \texttt{EnforcementAgent}, which serves as the operational extension tasked with physically monitoring household compliance and executing punitive measures. To operationalize the ``No Segregation, No Collection'' mandate, the \texttt{BarangayAgent} deploys these local enforcers using both base funds and Mayor Agent augmentations. 

To ensure ecological validity, each enforcement agent is initialized with a \texttt{base\_daily\_capacity} of 30 households. This cap reflects the geographical realities and administrative friction of monitoring the municipality. Operating on a quarterly time step of 66 standard working days, the maximum theoretical inspection capacity ($C_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}$) of an active enforcer as defined in Equation \ref{eq:cost_enforcement}, where $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}$ represents an efficiency multiplier (defaulted to 1.0) that can be adjusted to simulate varying levels of institutional competence.

The physical environment of the Agent-Based Model (ABM) is constructed as a two-dimensional spatial grid, where Household and Enforcement Agents are geographically distributed to represent the topographical layout of the municipality. Unlike centralized, equation-based models that assume uniform mixing or omniscient actors, this spatial formulation enforces strict geographical limitations on agent interactions \citep{Meng2018}. Specifically, Enforcement Agents do not possess global, omniscient vision of the entire barangay. Instead, they operate under bounded rationality and physical constraints, forcing them to rely on localized spatial searches within a defined line-of-sight (e.g., $Radius = 10$). This architectural design accurately simulates the limited monitoring capacity of real-world local enforcers who can only act upon non-compliant behaviors physically observed within their immediate vicinity \citep{Dalugdog2021, TianReview2024}.

The algorithmic movement of the Enforcement Agents is bifurcated into two distinct behavioral states: ``Chase Mode'' and ``Patrol Mode.'' When in Patrol Mode, the agent conducts a stochastic spatial search, moving to random adjacent coordinates. This represents the routine, unguided neighborhood rounds conducted by personnel when no immediate waste violations are visible. However, if a non-compliant Household Agent is detected within the enforcer's scanning radius, the agent dynamically switches to Chase Mode, calculating a directed spatial trajectory toward the closest violator to execute an intervention \citep{Zhao2022}. 

During each simulation step, the deployed agent employs this randomized monitoring strategy, selecting a discrete sample of households up to its capacity limit. If a household is non-compliant, the agent flags it and increments its internal \texttt{citations\_issued} tracker, directly triggering the financial penalty ($F$) within the household's utility function. 


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{The Enforcer's Daily Patrol and Fining Process}
\label{alg:enforcement_patrol_english}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Starting Information Needed:} The Enforcer's current location, and a ``Field of Vision'' covering the surrounding 10 blocks (or units)
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 1: Mark the Immediate Area}
\State Mark all households right next to the Enforcer as ``Visited'' (so the Enforcer doesn't get stuck checking the exact same houses over and over)
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 2: Decide Where to Go Next (Movement Logic)}
\State Look around the entire Field of Vision for households that haven't been visited yet
\State Out of those unvisited households, spot anyone who is currently breaking the rules (Non-Compliant)
\Statex
\If{there are compliant in sight}
    \State \textit{CHASE}
    \State Pick the non-compliant who is closest to the Enforcer
    \State Take one step directly toward that specific household
\Else
    \State \textit{PATROL}
    \State If no one is breaking the rules nearby, take a step in a random direction to continue patrolling the neighborhood
\EndIf
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 3: Issue Fines (Enforcement Execution)}
\State Look at the households immediately surrounding the Enforcer's new location (the ``Catch Zone'')
\For{every household in this immediate catch area}
    \If{the household is actively breaking the rules}
        \State Issue a fine to the household (this physically decreases their money/happiness and hurts their attitude toward the government)
        \State Add the fine amount to the city's overall collected fines tracker
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


Crucially, the presence of these agents generates a systemic feedback loop. Following inspections, the enforcement agent updates the barangay's macro-level Perceived Probability of Detection ($P_{\mathrm{Detection}}$) using the ratio of total inspection capacity to the total household population:

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{Detection}} = \min\left(1.0, \frac{\sum C_{\mathrm{Quarterly}}}{N_{\mathrm{Households}}}\right)
    \label{eq:prob_detection}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Macro-level Perceived Probability of Detection}

This updated probability is broadcasted back to the \texttt{HouseholdAgent} objects, dynamically increasing their anticipated risk. This spatial limitation profoundly reflects the real-world logistical constraints of municipal governance, particularly the deployment of \textit{barangay tanods} (village watchmen) or local environmental enforcers in the Philippines. Local Government Units (LGUs) rarely have the manpower or budgetary resources to monitor every household simultaneously \citep{Abordo2025}. Consequently, the probability of a household being caught and fined is not absolute; rather, it is a highly localized phenomenon dependent on spatial proximity and the enforcer's current patrol route. This localized enforcement mechanism organically generates the dynamic, fluctuating detection probability experienced by households, ensuring that the deterrent effect of punitive policies is realistically constrained by the physical and temporal capacity of the barangay's law enforcement \citep{Jimenez2025}.

\subsection{Household Agent Design: The TPB Framework and Utility Architecture}

The decision-making architecture of the \texttt{HouseholdAgent} is governed by a dynamic utility function grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which posits that an individual's intention to perform a behavior is a function of their attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control \citep{Ceschi2021}. In contrast to static behavioral models, this framework incorporates time-variant weights ($w(t)$) for psychological constructs, allowing agent behavior to evolve non-linearly in response to municipal interventions and social feedback loops \citep{Taraghi2025, Ma2023}. The core decision logic is represented by the utility of segregation ($U_{\mathrm{segregate}}$):

\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{segregate}} = (w_A(t) A) + (w_{SN}(t) SN_{\mathrm{local}}) + (w_{PBC}(t)  PBC) - C_{\mathrm{Net}} + \epsilon
    \label{eq:utility_function}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Household Utility of Segregation Function}

In this formulation, $w_A(t)$ represents the temporal evolution of the agent's internal valuation of segregation, functioning as a decay model that increases in response to Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) investment and decays stochastically in the absence of reinforcement to simulate ``public forgetting'' \citep{Trushna2024}. The social component, $SN_{\mathrm{local}}$, is an endogenous variable derived from the observed compliance rate of the agent's immediate spatial neighborhood, capturing the effects of social pressure and observational learning \citep{Meng2018, Liao2024}. To account for inherent uncertainty in human decision-making, $\epsilon$ introduces stochastic noise ($\mu=0, \sigma=0.05$), ensuring the model reflects real-world behavioral variance \citep{Subedi2025}.

A critical innovation in this model is the calculation of $C_{\mathrm{Net}}$, the net behavioral cost, which characterizes the perceived friction of compliance:

\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{Net}} = C_{\mathrm{Effort}} + (\gamma C_{\mathrm{Monetary}}) - (\gamma I) - (\gamma F P_{\mathrm{Detection}})
    \label{eq:net_disutility}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Net Disutility of Segregation Function}

\noindent This cost-benefit sub-model integrates both physical and financial barriers, defined as follows:

\begin{description}
    \item[$\bm{C_{\mathrm{Effort}}}$:] The physical hassle associated with washing, sorting, and storage of waste.
    \item[$\bm{C_{\mathrm{Monetary}}}$:] Tangible financial expenses, such as procuring color-coded bins or sacks.
    \item[$\bm{\gamma}$ (Income Sensitivity):] A weighting factor derived from household income level; for lower-income households, $\gamma > 1$ as financial levers ($I$ and $F$) carry greater psychological weight, whereas $\gamma < 1$ for higher-income households.
    \item[$\bm{I}$ and $\bm{F}$:] The objective magnitudes of monetary incentives and punitive fines, respectively.
    \item[$\bm{P_{\mathrm{Detection}}}$:] The likelihood of enforcement detection, dynamically linked to the Mayor Agent's resource allocation.
\end{description}

The model also captures complex psychological feedbacks such as ``psychological reactance.'' While the Mayor Agent's investments generally improve Attitude ($A$) and Subjective Norms ($SN$) \citep{Trushna2024}, if enforcement intensity crosses a specific threshold, Attitude may paradoxically decrease. This reflects the tendency of individuals to resist coercive mandates when they perceive a loss of autonomy \citep{LoanBalanay2023}. Finally, Perceived Behavioral Control ($PBC$) is calibrated by the agent's internal psychological friction, acknowledging that agents with strong motivation may still fail to comply if the behavior is highly disruptive to established routines.

\subsubsection{Behavioral Hysteresis: Social Shields and Norm Internalization}

Standard Agent-Based Models often suffer from hyper-reactivity, exhibiting unrealistic behavioral decay where compliance collapses immediately upon the cessation of external stimuli. To more accurately simulate the real-world stability of established habits, this study incorporates a ``Two-Stage Social Shield'' and \textit{Norm Internalization Mechanism}. This mathematically models human behavioral inertia—or ``Cultural Inertia''—where a behavior transitions from a calculated, incentive-driven decision to an internalized social habit once critical thresholds are reached \citep{Ceschi2021, Corcoran2020}.

To achieve this, the standard attitude update function is refined by a dynamic damping factor ($D_{\mathrm{factor}}$), suggesting that established social norms act as a buffer against the rapid erosion of compliance \citep{Andre2021}:

\begin{equation}
    A_{t+1} = A_t - (\delta \times D_{\mathrm{factor}})
\end{equation}
\addequation{Attitude Decay and Damping Function}

The damping factor is governed by the strength of local social norms ($SN$) through two distinct stages:

\textbf{Stage 1: Bayanihan Activation (The 40\% Floor):} Triggered when barangay compliance surpasses 40\%, representing an emergent community spirit that slows the erosion of positive norms. This acts as a \textit{Social Norm Floor}, ensuring that once a baseline awareness is achieved, perceived social pressure does not drop to zero, representing residual collective memory \citep{Nishimura2022}.

\textbf{Stage 2: Norm Lock-in (The 70\% Tipping Point):} Occurs when compliance exceeds 70\%, representing deep cultural habituation where segregation becomes the default state \citep{Jimenez2025}. This reflects ``critical mass'' theory, where minority behaviors cascade into majority conventions \citep{Centola2018, Nyborg2024}. 

Mathematically, this damping effect is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{factor}} = 
    \begin{cases} 
        0.1 & \text{if } SN > 0.70 \text{ (Stage 2: Strong Norms / Internalized)} \\
        0.5 & \text{if } SN > 0.40 \text{ (Stage 1: Bayanihan Activation)} \\
        1.0 & \text{otherwise (Weak Norms / Standard Decay)}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\addequation{Dynamic Damping Factor for Behavioral Inertia}

This refinement is critical for addressing sustainability objectives, allowing the Mayor Agent to discover strategies that foster self-sustaining compliance rather than perpetual reliance on high-cost incentives.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic Implementation of Household Decisions}

The integration of the dynamic utility function and the protective social shields is executed daily by each \texttt{HouseholdAgent} via Algorithm \ref{alg:household_decision_english}. 

If the calculated $Utility$ exceeds $0.0$, the agent's baseline intent is to segregate. However, intentionality does not guarantee perfect execution \citep{McCulloch2022}. To model this, the algorithm implements a strict ``1\% Human Error'' rule. Even when a household has a highly positive utility and full intention to comply, there is a 1\% probability that they fail to segregate on any given day due to unpredictable real-world friction (e.g., misplacing items, lack of time, or full bins). This constraint prevents the model from plateauing at an unrealistic, deterministic 100\% compliance rate, ensuring ecological validity aligned with actual municipal waste management data \citep{TianReview2024}.

\vspace*{-2cm} 
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{The Household's Daily Decision Process: To Segregate or Not?}
\label{alg:household_decision_english}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Starting Information Needed:} The Household's Income, their baseline Motivation, their susceptibility to Peer Pressure, and the Base Effort required to segregate waste.
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 1: Evaluate Peer Pressure and "Sticky" Habits}
\State Look at immediate neighbors to see what percentage are following the rules
\State Add a "Respect for Authority" bonus if Enforcers are highly visible in the area
\State Calculate the household's ``Perceived Reality'' (what they think the neighborhood is doing), capped at a realistic 92\%
\Statex
\State \textit{The Habit Shield (Resistance to Negative Peer Pressure):}
\If{the neighborhood seems to be doing worse than the household previously thought}
    \State \textit{Apply the Habit Damper:}
    \If{they live in a highly compliant neighborhood (over 70\% passing)}
        \State They are very stubborn: they mostly hold onto their good habits and only slightly let the new negative reality affect them
    \ElsIf{they live in an average neighborhood (over 40\% passing)}
        \State They are moderately stubborn: they let the negative reality affect them a bit more
    \Else
        \State They live in a failing neighborhood: they instantly lose hope and adopt the negative reality
    \EndIf
\Else
    \State If the neighborhood is doing better than expected, they adopt the positive reality immediately
\EndIf
\State Cap their final sense of Peer Pressure at 95\% (since no one believes a neighborhood is 100\% perfect)
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 2: Update Internal Motivation (Attitude)}
\State Motivation naturally fades over time, but this decay slows down if they live in a compliant neighborhood
\State Boost their motivation if they recently heard an Information/Educational Campaign
\State Adjust for human psychology (e.g., feeling Pride for doing a good job, or getting Complacent if things seem too easy)
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 3: Weigh the Costs vs. Benefits (The Final Choice)}
\State Calculate the Financial Impact: Weigh the potential municipal rewards against the risk of getting caught and fined
\State Calculate the ``Net Effort'': How hard is it to segregate? (Keep in mind that rich and poor households value the exact same fine differently)
\State Add a tiny bit of random human unpredictability to their thought process
\Statex
\State \textit{Make the Decision:}
\If{the overall benefits and pressures outweigh the effort}
    \State The household decides to Comply (segregate their waste)
    \Statex
    \State \textit{Human Error}
    \If{even with the best intentions, they roll a 1\% chance of making a mistake}
        \State They accidentally fail to segregate that day (e.g., they forgot, or a child mixed the bins)
    \EndIf
\Else
    \State The effort is too high or motivation is too low: The household decides NOT to comply
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Global Sensitivity Analysis: Addressing Parameter Uncertainty}

Agent-Based Models (ABMs) are highly effective at simulating complex socio-environmental systems, but they frequently suffer from parameter uncertainty, particularly when dealing with unobservable psychological constructs and heuristic behavioral rules \citep{McCulloch2022}. To rigorously validate the structural integrity of the simulation and quantify this uncertainty, a variance-based Global Sensitivity Analysis (GSA) was conducted using the Sobol method \citep{Sobol2001}. 

Unlike local sensitivity analyses that alter one variable at a time, the Sobol method explores the entire multidimensional parameter space simultaneously. This sophisticated approach decomposes the variance of the model's primary macro-level outputs—specifically, the final Global Compliance rate and accumulated Political Capital—into fractions directly attributable to the individual Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) parameters and their complex, non-linear interactions \citep{ZhengAI2022}. By doing so, it provides a quantitative measure of how heavily the Mayor Agent's policy outcomes rely on specific assumptions about human behavior.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Determining What Matters Most (Stress-Testing the Simulation)}
\label{alg:sobol_gsa_english}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Starting Information Needed:} The Household Agents, and the Total Number of "What-If" Scenarios to test (e.g., thousands of combinations)
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 1: Define the "What-If" Variables}
\State Select the 5 key psychological traits we want to stress-test, and define their realistic minimum and maximum limits:
\State \quad - \textit{Personal Attitude:} How heavily households weigh their own internal motivation
\State \quad - \textit{Peer Pressure:} How heavily they weigh the actions of their neighbors
\State \quad - \textit{Perceived Control:} How much they care about the difficulty of the rules
\State \quad - \textit{Physical Effort:} The actual baseline effort required to segregate waste
\State \quad - \textit{Motivation Decay:} How quickly a household naturally loses its good habits
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 2: Create the Test Scenarios}
\State Generate a massive list of unique test cases by scrambling and mixing these 5 traits together into thousands of different mathematical combinations
\State Prepare empty record books to track the final results of each test
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 3: Run the Simulation for Every Single Scenario}
\For{every unique combination of traits in our massive list}
    \State Inject these specific psychological traits into the simulation's household agent
    \State Start the simulation using the standard Pure IEC policy strategy (Status Quo Policy)
    \Statex
    \State \textit{Let the simulation play out:}
    \State Fast-forward the simulation until it reaches the end of its timeline
    \Statex
    \State \textit{Record the Final Grades:}
    \State Check the municipality's final Waste Segregation Compliance Rate
    \State Check the Mayor Agent's final Political Capital score
    \State Write both scores down in the record books next to that specific test case
\EndFor
\Statex
\State \textbf{Step 4: Calculate the "Impact Scores" (The Sobol Method)}
\State Feed the massive record books into the statistical analyzer
\State Calculate which specific psychological traits were actually responsible for causing the municipality's compliance to succeed or fail
\Statex
\State \textbf{Final Output:} A report showing the ``Direct Impact'' (which trait changes outcomes immediately on its own) and the ``Ripple Effect'' (which trait changes outcomes when it interacts with other traits) for all 5 variables.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The parameter space was deliberately bounded to reflect plausible ranges of human behavioral drivers derived from existing socio-psychological literature \citep{Meng2018}. The psychological weights for Attitude ($w_a$), Subjective Norms ($w_{sn}$), and Perceived Behavioral Control ($w_{pbc}$) were uniformly bounded between $[0.1, 0.6]$. This wide yet constrained range ensures that no single psychological factor deterministically overpowers the decision-making equation without empirical justification, allowing the simulation to explore a diverse spectrum of household archetypes (e.g., highly conformist versus highly independent citizens). Additionally, operational constraints such as Base Effort ($c_{effort}$) and Attitude Decay were bounded at $[0.1, 0.5]$ and $[0.001, 0.02]$, respectively. 

By calculating the First-Order ($S_1$) and Total-Order ($S_T$) indices, we can isolate which specific parameters most heavily dictate systemic outcomes. Identifying these highly sensitive parameters is crucial; it not only highlights where future empirical data collection efforts by the municipality should be focused to reduce real-world uncertainty, but it also mathematically validates whether the emergent behavioral dynamics of the ABM align with established psychological theories of waste segregation \citep{TianReview2024}.
