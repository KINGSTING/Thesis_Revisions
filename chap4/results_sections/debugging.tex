Following the calibration of the Agent-Based Model (ABM), the study encountered significant computational and behavioral challenges during the initial training phase of the Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent. These challenges necessitated critical architectural deviations from the initial thesis proposal, specifically regarding the reinforcement learning framework, the reward optimization algorithm, and the mathematical stability of the household agents. This section details the quantitative results that justified the transition to the Heuristic-Guided (HuDRL) framework and the integration of a ``Social Norm Shield'' to prevent unrealistic compliance volatility.

\subsection{Overcoming the Sparse Reward Problem via Action Masking}
Even with an interactive environment, a fundamental challenge emerged: the \textit{Sparse Reward Problem} inherent to the high-dimensional continuous action space ($d=21$). The initial assumption was that a standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent could autonomously discover the optimal ``Sequential Saturation'' strategy given sufficient training episodes. 

In the baseline non-heuristic implementation, the unguided neural network was required to simultaneously optimize continuous budget allocations across three distinct policy levers for seven heterogeneous barangays. The probability of the agent randomly initializing a policy that concentrated massive resources on a single struggling barangay was mathematically negligible. Instead, the baseline PPO agent rapidly converged to a local optimum characterized by "safety-seeking" behavior. It learned to distribute funds equally across the grid to minimize the risk of triggering the Political Collapse penalty, resulting in heavily diluted interventions that completely failed to overcome the citizens' behavioral inertia \citep{ZhengAI2022}. 

To resolve this, the methodology transitioned to the Heuristic-Guided (HuDRL) framework. As detailed in the system architecture, this involved introducing a Temperature-Scaled Softmax function ($\tau=5.0$) to violently amplify the AI's highest confidences, forcing polarized budget concentrations. Furthermore, a Safety-Critical Action Mask was integrated—specifically the 70\% Graduation Rule and the 50\% Phase-Shift Crisis Guardrail—which programmatically blocked the AI from wasting funds on stable areas or starving critical sectors during its random exploration phase.

\subsection{Heuristic Reward Design and Population Density Bias}
During the refinement of the HuDRL reward structure, a critical design choice involved defining the optimal heuristic rule to guide the agent's spatial targeting. This necessitated a series of trial benchmarks comparing two competing philosophies of resource allocation. The first approach, termed Algorithm 1 or ``Maximin Triage,'' penalized the agent purely based on the barangay exhibiting the lowest absolute percentage of compliance, effectively blinding the AI to the specific household population size of each jurisdiction. In contrast, Algorithm 2 or ``Density-Weighted Triage'' factored in both low compliance and high household population density. This second approach operated on the theoretical assumption that targeting densely populated urban centers would maximize the raw number of compliant households, and thus the total environmental impact, per budget cycle.

Empirical testing, however, revealed a severe algorithmic flaw in Algorithm 2 rooted in what \cite{Mehrabi2021} defines as ``population density bias.'' Because the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) objective function seeks to maximize cumulative scalar rewards, the agent discovered a mathematical exploit within the density-weighted environment. It developed a learned propensity to disproportionately funnel municipal funds into the massive urban center of Barangay Poblacion ($N=1,534$), even when that area’s compliance was only marginally below optimal levels. From the AI's perspective, shifting a massive population by merely 2\% yielded a higher absolute numerical reward than the difficult task of fully stabilizing a sparsely populated rural barangay, such as Babalaya ($N=171$), even if the latter was failing at a critical 10\% compliance level.

This behavior generated severe systemic inequality within the simulation, as the agent continuously harvested ``cheap'' marginal rewards from high-density areas while abandoning rural nodes in the Collapse Zone indefinitely. This computational phenomenon perfectly mirrors recognized failures in real-world algorithmic public policy, where optimizing strictly for aggregate efficiency reinforces systemic inequities against low-density populations \citep{Alphonso2024}. To resolve this reward exploitation, the final HuDRL framework adopted the Maximin Triage of Algorithm 1. By blinding the agent to density, the heuristic compels the AI to execute systematic ``bottleneck resolution'' \citep{TianReview2024}. This forces the AI Mayor to prioritize the most vulnerable, lowest-performing sectors regardless of their size, ensuring that municipal-wide sustainability is achieved through equitable progress rather than aggregate statistical inflation.

\subsection{Addressing Behavioral Volatility via the Social Norm Shield}
The final major algorithmic refinement addressed the behavioral stability of the \texttt{HouseholdAgents}, specifically targeting a phenomenon identified in early headless simulations as ``Instantaneous Collapse.'' In these preliminary runs, compliance rates in a stabilized barangay would frequently plummet from 90\% to near 0\% within a single quarter the moment the Mayor Agent withdrew its strike force or incentive budget. While this volatility was mathematically faithful to the raw linear decay equations of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), it was sociologically unrealistic. Real-world populations typically exhibit cultural hysteresis, where established habits persist long after the removal of external financial or punitive stimuli \citep{Nishimura2022}.

To correct this computational artifact, the study implemented a ``Social Norm Shield'' governed by two empirically tuned thresholds designed to simulate the non-linear phase transitions characteristic of collective behavior models \citep{Centola2018}. The first critical threshold is the Tipping Point, set at 70\% compliance. The model assumes that once a localized jurisdiction breaches this level, the community’s behavior transitions from being primarily ``incentive-driven'' to ``norm-driven.'' Upon reaching this point, the simulation executes a deterministic hard-lock on the Subjective Norm ($w_{sn}$) variable, effectively preventing standard dynamic decay. This mechanism ensures that social conventions do not instantly erode without constant government funding, a choice that aligns with the ``ratchet effect'' observed in environmental sociology where compliance becomes an internalized community standard \citep{Andre2021, Nyborg2024}.

Complementing this tipping point is the Social Norm Floor, established at 40\% compliance. This absolute bound was implemented to prevent total mathematical collapse during the DRL agent's early exploration phase, ensuring that spatial peer pressure and internalized ``moral obligation'' maintain a baseline level of compliance even in the absence of active enforcement \citep{Zheng2022}. Collectively, these refinements were mathematically paramount to the agent's success. By enabling cultural hysteresis through these thresholds, the HuDRL agent could successfully execute its discovered ``Sequential Saturation'' strategy. It allowed the AI Mayor to concentrate municipal capital to conquer a specific barangay, lock in its social norms via the shield, and then safely relocate the budget to the next target without the previous jurisdiction collapsing behind it \citep{Abdallah2021}. This transitioned the optimization task from a perpetual struggle against decay to a systematic expansion of stable, high-compliance nodes.
