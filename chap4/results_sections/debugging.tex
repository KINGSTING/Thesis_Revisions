Following the calibration of the Agent-Based Model (ABM), the study encountered significant computational and behavioral challenges during the initial training phase of the Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent. These challenges necessitated critical architectural deviations from the initial thesis proposal, specifically regarding the reinforcement learning framework, the reward optimization algorithm, and the mathematical stability of the household agents. This section details the quantitative results that justified the transition to the Heuristic-Guided (HuDRL) framework and the integration of a ``Social Norm Shield'' to prevent unrealistic compliance volatility.

\subsection{Overcoming the Sparse Reward Problem via Action Masking}
Even with an interactive environment, a fundamental challenge emerged: the \textit{Sparse Reward Problem} inherent to the high-dimensional continuous action space ($d=21$). The initial assumption was that a standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent could autonomously discover the optimal ``Sequential Saturation'' strategy given sufficient training episodes. 

In the baseline non-heuristic implementation, the unguided neural network was required to simultaneously optimize continuous budget allocations across three distinct policy levers for seven heterogeneous barangays. The probability of the agent randomly initializing a policy that concentrated massive resources on a single struggling barangay was mathematically negligible. Instead, the baseline PPO agent rapidly converged to a local optimum characterized by "safety-seeking" behavior. It learned to distribute funds equally across the grid to minimize the risk of triggering the Political Collapse penalty, resulting in heavily diluted interventions that completely failed to overcome the citizens' behavioral inertia \citep{ZhengAI2022}. 

To resolve this, the methodology transitioned to the Heuristic-Guided (HuDRL) framework. As detailed in the system architecture, this involved introducing a Temperature-Scaled Softmax function ($\tau=5.0$) to violently amplify the AI's highest confidences, forcing polarized budget concentrations. Furthermore, a Safety-Critical Action Mask was integrated—specifically the 70\% Graduation Rule and the 50\% Phase-Shift Crisis Guardrail—which programmatically blocked the AI from wasting funds on stable areas or starving critical sectors during its random exploration phase.

\subsection{Comparative Convergence and Algorithmic Robustness}

To evaluate the overall efficacy of the methodological refinements, the simulation benchmarked the Heuristic-Guided Deep Reinforcement Learning (HuDRL) architecture against a Vanilla (Non-Heuristic) PPO approach and several static allocation baselines over a 12-quarter operational horizon.

The Vanilla PPO represented by the dashed trajectory exhibited severe policy stagnation. Throughout the entire three-year simulation timeline, the global compliance rate remained virtually flat at approximately 20\%. This performance consistently trapped the municipality within the ``Collapse Zone.'' We operationally define this zone as a state-space trap where an unguided agent cannot reliably trigger the 70\% tipping-point step-rewards \citep{Centola2018, Constantino2022} and subsequently suffers gradient collapse, stagnating at a mean reward of 1859.43. Although foundational Deep Reinforcement Learning has achieved superhuman control in highly structured environments \citep{Popov2017, Silver2016}, Vanilla PPO failed to escape this zone. This demonstrates that without heuristic action-masking or adaptive triage logic, an unguided system fails to concentrate enough capital to incentivize actual behavioral change in a sparse-reward landscape \citep{Cheng2021, Vecerik2018, ZhengAI2022}.

In stark contrast, the \textit{Heuristic-Guided} approach demonstrated rapid and stable gradient convergence toward optimal compliance. Starting at a baseline compliance rate of 51.8\% in Quarter 1 (under the initial guided framework), the agent successfully navigated the non-convex policy space, breaching the critical 70\% Stability Threshold by Quarter 2. By systematically targeting failing sectors via the algorithmic guardrails, the system achieved near-saturation by Quarter 6 (approximately 98\% compliance) and maintained asymptotic stability through Quarter 12. This success directly proves that the DRL agent must be explicitly constrained by heuristic rules to operationalize the "Sequential Saturation" strategy, rather than being expected to independently discover a computationally viable policy from scratch \citep{Cheng2021, Nair2018}.

\subsection{Ablation Study: Isolating the Effect of PPO}
\label{subsec:ablation}

To isolate the contribution of the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent, we compare its cumulative reward against several non‑learning or ablated baselines. The reward signal is the primary optimization objective in this study (see Section~3.4.4). It is a composite metric that directly rewards compliance gains, penalizes wasted funds, encourages focus on struggling barangays, and incorporates political capital sustainability. Because the reward function is tightly aligned with the LGU’s operational goals, it serves as a valid proxy for overall policy quality. Table~\ref{tab:comparative_reward} reports the mean cumulative reward over five independent seeds for each condition.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random:} Uniform random allocation across all 21 policy levers (7 barangays × 3 instruments). Represents the lowest performance floor with no structure.
    \item \textbf{Dirichlet:} Random allocation using a Dirichlet distribution, which occasionally produces concentrated budgets. Tests whether any stochastic structure improves over pure uniform randomness.
    \item \textbf{Greedy:} A deterministic heuristic that allocates the entire quarterly budget to the barangay with the lowest current compliance, split equally among enforcement, incentives, and IEC. This isolates the effect of the \textit{sequential saturation heuristic} without any learning.
    \item \textbf{Vanilla DRL:} Standard PPO trained \textit{without} any heuristic action masking (no 70\% graduation rule, no target‑swap, no crisis guardrail). This tests whether the RL agent can learn a useful policy from scratch in this sparse‑reward environment.
    \item \textbf{HuDRL:} The full proposed system (heuristic‑guided PPO).
\end{itemize}

HuDRL achieves the highest mean reward (10555.0) with extremely low variance (\(\sigma = 4.0\)). A Welch’s \(t\)-test comparing HuDRL to the Greedy baseline yields \(p < 0.001\) with a very large effect size (Cohen’s \(d = 139.11\)). The Greedy heuristic itself performs substantially better than random allocation (mean reward 10094.5 vs. 9387.8 for Random), confirming that the sequential saturation heuristic is powerful even without learning. However, the learned policy (HuDRL) improves upon Greedy by approximately 460 reward points, demonstrating that adaptive timing, dynamic blending of policy levers, and the ability to switch to maintenance mode provide additional value that a static rule cannot achieve.

Critically, Vanilla DRL (PPO without heuristics) collapses to a mean reward of only 1859.4, with high variance (\(\sigma = 156.8\)) and frequent political collapse (observed in training logs). This confirms that heuristic guidance is not merely helpful but \textit{essential} for the RL agent to overcome the sparse reward problem in this high‑dimensional action space (21 continuous levers). Without the safety‑critical action mask and the phased guardrails, the agent cannot concentrate sufficient resources to trigger the social tipping point and instead converges to a local optimum of inaction.

The ablation study isolates three distinct effects:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The sequential saturation heuristic provides a strong baseline (Greedy).
    \item Adding learning (HuDRL) significantly improves upon that baseline, yielding higher reward and comparable stability.
    \item Removing heuristics entirely (Vanilla DRL) leads to complete failure, proving that heuristic guidance is necessary for effective exploration in this domain.
\end{enumerate}
Thus, the PPO agent contributes both performance gains and robustness beyond the best non‑learning strategy. The reward differences are statistically significant and practically large, confirming that the learning component is an essential part of the HuDRL framework.

\subsection*{4.3.4 | Performance Evaluation and Sensitivity Analysis}

To evaluate the HuDRL agent, we define four metrics aligned with the LGU’s objectives (see Section~3.5.5). All values are derived directly from results already presented. The first metric is cumulative reward, the primary optimization objective described in Section~3.4.4. From Table~\ref{tab:comparative_reward}, HuDRL achieves a mean reward of 10555.0 with a standard deviation of only 4.0, which is significantly higher than the Greedy heuristic (10094.5) and vastly superior to Vanilla DRL (1859.4). The second metric is terminal compliance, the population‑weighted compliance rate at Quarter~12. Table~\ref{tab:policy_comparison} shows that HuDRL reaches 90.45\%, compared to only 30.18\% for the Status Quo and 15.91\% for Pure Incentives; Pure Enforcement collapses completely in Quarter~2. The third metric is budget efficiency, defined as compliance gain per P100,000 spent. Using the initial municipality‑wide compliance of 12.5\% (Section~4.1.2) and a total three‑year municipal budget of 4.5 million pesos, HuDRL yields a gain of (90.45 – 12.5) = 77.95 percentage points over 45 units of P100k, or 1.73\% per P100k. The Status Quo achieves only 0.39\% per P100k, and Pure Incentives 0.08\% (Pure Enforcement is not applicable because it terminates early). Thus, HuDRL is the most cost‑effective strategy. The fourth metric is stability, measured by reward variance across random seeds (Table~\ref{tab:comparative_reward}) and the occurrence of political collapse. HuDRL exhibits near‑zero variance ($\sigma^2 = 16$), whereas Random ($\sigma^2 \approx 572,000$) and Vanilla DRL ($\sigma^2 \approx 24,600$) are highly volatile. Moreover, political collapse (Section~4.4.2) occurs only for Pure Enforcement; HuDRL never collapses.

The model’s sensitivity to key parameters is already examined through two complementary analyses presented earlier in this thesis. First, the Global Sensitivity Analysis (Section~4.2, Table~\ref{tab:sobol_analysis}) uses the Sobol method over 10,000 parameter samples to decompose variance in compliance outcomes. It reveals that the Cost of Effort ($C_{\text{Effort}}$) alone accounts for 79\% of the variance, while Attitude weight ($w_A$) and Decay rate ($\delta$) have near‑zero direct sensitivity. This implies that, within the simulation, the HuDRL policy is most sensitive to physical friction. If the effort required for segregation were substantially higher than our calibrated range (for example, if collection infrastructure deteriorates or households lack basic bins), the model predicts that performance would degrade sharply – this is a clear point of fragility. Second, the Social Tipping Point Robustness test (Section~4.2.3, Table~\ref{tab:robustness_results}) varies the social norm lock‑in threshold $T_{\text{high}}$ from 0.60 to 0.85, a 25\% range. Under the HuDRL policy, terminal compliance remains remarkably stable, fluctuating only between 89.83\% and 91.84\%. This demonstrates that the discovered Sequential Saturation strategy is not tuned to a specific threshold; the RL agent adapts by sustaining interventions longer when the population is more resistant. Overall, the only clear fragility identified is the Cost of Effort. No other parameter – attitude, social norms, decay rate, or tipping point – causes major performance swings within the tested ranges.

The performance evaluation confirms that the HuDRL agent optimises cumulative reward, terminal compliance, and budget efficiency while maintaining near‑zero variance and avoiding political collapse. The sensitivity analysis shows that the policy is robust to variations in social thresholds and most behavioural parameters, with physical effort being the dominant and most fragile lever. These findings directly support the operational recommendations for the LGU: prioritise friction‑reducing interventions, and adopt a phased, adaptive allocation strategy that is resilient to changes in community responsiveness.

\subsection{Heuristic Reward Design and Population Density Bias}
During the refinement of the HuDRL reward structure, a critical design choice involved defining the optimal heuristic rule to guide the agent's spatial targeting. This necessitated a series of trial benchmarks comparing two competing philosophies of resource allocation. The first approach, termed Algorithm 1 or ``Maximin Triage,'' penalized the agent purely based on the barangay exhibiting the lowest absolute percentage of compliance, effectively blinding the AI to the specific household population size of each jurisdiction. In contrast, Algorithm 2 or ``Density-Weighted Triage'' factored in both low compliance and high household population density. This second approach operated on the theoretical assumption that targeting densely populated urban centers would maximize the raw number of compliant households, and thus the total environmental impact, per budget cycle.

Empirical testing, however, revealed a severe algorithmic flaw in Algorithm 2 rooted in what \cite{Mehrabi2021} defines as ``population density bias.'' Because the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) objective function seeks to maximize cumulative scalar rewards, the agent discovered a mathematical exploit within the density-weighted environment. It developed a learned propensity to disproportionately funnel municipal funds into the massive urban center of Barangay Poblacion ($N=1,534$), even when that area’s compliance was only marginally below optimal levels. From the AI's perspective, shifting a massive population by merely 2\% yielded a higher absolute numerical reward than the difficult task of fully stabilizing a sparsely populated rural barangay, such as Babalaya ($N=171$), even if the latter was failing at a critical 10\% compliance level.

This behavior generated severe systemic inequality within the simulation, as the agent continuously harvested ``cheap'' marginal rewards from high-density areas while abandoning rural nodes in the Collapse Zone indefinitely. This computational phenomenon perfectly mirrors recognized failures in real-world algorithmic public policy, where optimizing strictly for aggregate efficiency reinforces systemic inequities against low-density populations \citep{Alphonso2024}. To resolve this reward exploitation, the final HuDRL framework adopted the Maximin Triage of Algorithm 1. By blinding the agent to density, the heuristic compels the AI to execute systematic ``bottleneck resolution'' \citep{TianReview2024}. This forces the AI Mayor to prioritize the most vulnerable, lowest-performing sectors regardless of their size, ensuring that municipal-wide sustainability is achieved through equitable progress rather than aggregate statistical inflation.

\subsection{Addressing Behavioral Volatility via the Social Norm Shield}
The final major algorithmic refinement addressed the behavioral stability of the \texttt{HouseholdAgents}, specifically targeting a phenomenon identified in early headless simulations as ``Instantaneous Collapse.'' In these preliminary runs, compliance rates in a stabilized barangay would frequently plummet from 90\% to near 0\% within a single quarter the moment the Mayor Agent withdrew its strike force or incentive budget. While this volatility was mathematically faithful to the raw linear decay equations of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), it was sociologically unrealistic. Real-world populations typically exhibit cultural hysteresis, where established habits persist long after the removal of external financial or punitive stimuli \citep{Nishimura2022}.

To correct this computational artifact, the study implemented a ``Social Norm Shield'' governed by two empirically tuned thresholds designed to simulate the non-linear phase transitions characteristic of collective behavior models \citep{Centola2018}. The first critical threshold is the Tipping Point, set at 70\% compliance. The model assumes that once a localized jurisdiction breaches this level, the community’s behavior transitions from being primarily ``incentive-driven'' to ``norm-driven.'' Upon reaching this point, the simulation executes a deterministic hard-lock on the Subjective Norm ($w_{sn}$) variable, effectively preventing standard dynamic decay. This mechanism ensures that social conventions do not instantly erode without constant government funding, a choice that aligns with the ``ratchet effect'' observed in environmental sociology where compliance becomes an internalized community standard \citep{Nyborg2024}.

Complementing this tipping point is the Social Norm Floor, established at 40\% compliance. This absolute bound was implemented to prevent total mathematical collapse during the DRL agent's early exploration phase, ensuring that spatial peer pressure and internalized ``moral obligation'' maintain a baseline level of compliance even in the absence of active enforcement \citep{Zheng2022}. Collectively, these refinements were mathematically paramount to the agent's success. By enabling cultural hysteresis through these thresholds, the HuDRL agent could successfully execute its discovered ``Sequential Saturation'' strategy. It allowed the AI Mayor to concentrate municipal capital to conquer a specific barangay, lock in its social norms via the shield, and then safely relocate the budget to the next target without the previous jurisdiction collapsing behind it \citep{Abdallah2021}. This transitioned the optimization task from a perpetual struggle against decay to a systematic expansion of stable, high-compliance nodes.
