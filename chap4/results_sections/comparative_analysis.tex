\subsection{Baseline: The Status Quo}
To establish a comparative baseline, the simulation first evaluated the current administrative strategy employed by the Local Government Unit (LGU). Under this ``Status Quo'' regime, the Mayor Agent acts purely as a passive distributor, dividing the \textpeso 375,000 quarterly municipal budget equally among all seven barangays. As verified by the telemetry logs, this resulted in a fixed augmentation of exactly \textpeso 53,571.43 per node (14.3\% of the total budget), with 100\% of these municipal funds allocated strictly to Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) campaigns under the prevailing assumption that passive awareness is the primary driver of compliance.

The simulation results over the 12-quarter (3-year) horizon reveal a profound structural failure in this equal-distribution strategy. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:baseline_graph}, the Global Compliance Rate (represented by the black trajectory) suffered from severe stagnation, artificially ceiling at approximately 30.18\% by the end of the simulation. However, an analysis of the localized data reveals that this global stagnation is not the result of uniform municipal failure, but rather a violent computational bifurcation driven by Population Density Bias.

The success observed in the municipality's least populated sectors underscores the impact of over-funding in low-density environments. Barangays Mati ($N=165$), Babalaya ($N=171$), Demologan ($N=463$), and Binuni ($N=507$) all successfully breached the 70\% Social Tipping Point. Mati, for instance, surged from a 14.54\% initial compliance in Quarter 1 to an overwhelming 98.78\% by Quarter 12. Computationally, because these populations are relatively small, the fixed \textpeso 53k budget translated into a massive per-capita IEC intensity. This high intensity successfully overpowered the agents' baseline Cost of Effort ($c_{\text{effort}}$) and triggered the lock-in mechanism of the Social Norm Shield.

Conversely, the exact same \textpeso 53,571.43 allocation triggered a catastrophic behavioral collapse in the municipality's most heavily populated zones, illustrating the ``Urban Resource Trap.'' Barangay Liangan East ($N=608$) dropped from 14.14\% to 0.00\%, while the massive commercial center of Poblacion ($N=1,534$) flatlined at a negligible 0.06\%. In these sectors, the fixed budget was completely diluted across the large populations, resulting in a per-capita intervention intensity that was mathematically insufficient to overcome the physical and psychological friction of segregation. As a result, intrinsic motivation in these areas suffered from rapid decay ($\delta$).

Ultimately, the failure of the Status Quo quantitatively dismantles the administrative assumption that passive education, when applied uniformly, is sufficient for habit formation across diverse populations \citep{Abdallah2021}. More critically, the model demonstrates that an equal-distribution strategy acts as a severe dilution mechanism; by spreading resources evenly regardless of population density, the LGU achieved mathematical \textit{Equality} but failed entirely to achieve \textit{Equity}. Because the massive, failing populations of Poblacion and Liangan East statistically outweigh the successful rural sectors, they effectively anchored the entire municipality to a state of permanent non-compliance. This outcome aligns with the equity-efficiency tradeoff observed in algorithmic resource allocation \citep{Alphonso2024}, providing a clear justification for the dynamic, localized triage that the HuDRL agent was designed to perform.

\subsection{Pure Enforcement: The Draconian Trap and Political Collapse}
In the second scenario, the municipal budget was entirely diverted from Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) campaigns and channeled exclusively into the deployment of the Mayor's Municipal Strike Force. This strategy served as a computational test of the ``Deterrence Hypothesis,'' which posits that maximizing the probability of detection through severe policing is the most effective driver of compliance \citep{Chen2023}. Unlike the Status Quo's equal-distribution model, the telemetry data (\texttt{pure\_enforcement\_2}) revealed a population-weighted deployment strategy. For instance, the massive urban center of Barangay Poblacion received 50\% of the municipal budget (\textpeso 187,500), enabling the simultaneous deployment of 15 municipal inspectors, while rural barangays were allocated \textpeso 31,250 each to sustain two inspectors per node.

While the baseline Status Quo scenario spanned a full 12-quarter horizon, the Pure Enforcement simulation triggered a catastrophic system halt by the start of Quarter 3. The resulting data dismantles the viability of a purely punitive approach, despite an initial ``Fear Override'' that appeared overwhelmingly effective in the short term. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:pure_enforcement-graph}, the sudden influx of high-capacity inspectors triggered a violent deterrence spike, causing the Global Compliance Rate to surge from 8.43\% in Quarter 1 to 91.17\% by the second quarter. Within the model's logic, the sheer density of inspectors issuing \textpeso 1,000 citations mathematically overpowered the agents' Base Cost of Effort ($c_{\text{effort}}$), forcing the grid into a state of compliance driven strictly by financial self-preservation.

However, this physical success was offset by a simultaneous collapse in the agents' internal psychological matrices. The total absence of educational outreach, coupled with the relentless issuance of fines, triggered severe ``Perceived Unfairness'' penalties across the grid. This psychological degradation led to the fatal administrative flaw of the strategy: the rapid depletion of political legitimacy. Political Capital, which initialized at 1.0000, crashed to 0.3252 by the end of Quarter 2. As the simulation transitioned into Quarter 3, the continuous issuance of aggressive municipal fines breached the critical 0.10 survival threshold, triggering an event-driven interrupt within the \texttt{BacolodModel} architecture. This truncated outcome represents a profound finding within the optimization landscape, validating the argument that strictly punitive measures are sociologically toxic \citep{Badua2022}. While a ``Police State'' strategy can forcibly bridge the Intention-Action Gap and artificially trigger the 70\% Social Tipping Point, it burns through political capital faster than social norms can achieve the necessary lock-in effect. Consequently, the simulation demonstrates that brute-force enforcement is mathematically equivalent to a localized ``game over,'' as the AI Mayor cannot sustain the very compliance it created without losing the administrative authority to govern.

\subsection{Pure Incentives: The Transactional Trap and Budget Exhaustion}
The third scenario eliminated all municipal Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) and Enforcement funding, channeling 100\% of the Mayor Agent's budget into Tier 2 monetary rewards. This strategy tested a purely positive reinforcement model, evaluating whether financial subsidies alone could permanently overcome the physical friction ($c_{\text{effort}}$) of waste segregation. Unlike the Status Quo's equal distribution, the telemetry data (\texttt{pure\_incentives\_2}) revealed a population-weighted deployment. Barangay Poblacion, the massive urban center, received 50\% of the municipal budget (\textpeso 187,500), while the remaining six barangays received \textpeso 31,250 each. These funds were deposited directly into the barangays' cascading ledgers to augment local payouts.

As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:pure_incentives_graph}, the simulation results categorize the Pure Incentives strategy as a systemic failure. The Global Compliance Rate exhibited minimal movement, creeping from an initial 8.43\% to a stagnant 15.91\% by the end of the 12-quarter horizon. A granular analysis of the localized telemetry reveals that this stagnation was the product of a ``Transactional Trap'' characterized by rapid budget exhaustion and psychological backlash.

The primary driver of this stagnation was the mathematical impossibility of sustaining rewards for a large population, a phenomenon known as being a ``victim of success.'' At a fixed rate of \textpeso 500 per reward claim, the municipal augmentation funds proved insufficient to meet demand. In Barangay Poblacion ($N=1,534$), for example, the \textpeso 187,500 quarterly allocation could only subsidize 375 households. Once early adopters claimed these rewards, the Tier 2 ledger was instantly drained to zero, leaving the remaining 75\% of the population without compensation. This fiscal shortfall triggered a more destructive second phase: the execution of the ``Perceived Unfairness'' penalty. As the majority of households attempting to redeem their efforts were met with a \texttt{False} return from the cascading ledger, their internal motivation collapsed. This is statistically evident in the Global Summary telemetry, where the Average City Attitude ($w_a$) plummeted from 0.300 to a dismal 0.073, representing a catastrophic erosion of public trust in the municipal program.

Consequently, the simulation exhibited a bifurcated behavioral collapse where compliance relocation was most severe in the municipality's most vulnerable sectors. In low-income, agrarian jurisdictions like Barangay Demologan and Barangay Mati, compliance rates completely flatlined to 0.00\%. Citizens in these areas, lacking intrinsic motivation, refused to expend the high physical effort ($c_{\text{effort}}$) required for segregation without a guaranteed financial return. While Barangays Binuni (95.66\%) and Babalaya (87.72\%) emerged as the only successful nodes, their high performance was an artifact of their initialization parameters rather than the policy itself. Binuni's ``Wealthy Anomaly'' profile allowed its citizens to absorb the friction of segregation even when rewards ran dry, eventually allowing their baseline social norms to lock in once the 70\% tipping point was breached.

Ultimately, these results align with the sociological findings of \citet{Camarillo2021} and \citet{Zhao2022}, confirming that economic incentives create a fragile, transactional relationship between the state and the citizen. The model demonstrates that if monetary rewards are not strictly coupled with continuous IEC campaigns to build intrinsic value, behavior relay is inevitable once municipal liquidity is exhausted. A purely positive reinforcement model effectively bankrupted the LGU's fiscal reserves while simultaneously destroying the psychological foundation required for long-term sustainability.

As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:PPO_graph}, the HuDRL agent significantly outperformed all human-administrative baselines, successfully navigating the municipality out of the initial stagnation zone. The telemetry logs (\texttt{HuDRL\_2\_MAYOR\_INTERVENTION}) suggest that this superior performance is attributed to the discovery of a non-linear resource allocation logic termed ``Sequential Saturation.'' \subsection{The Dilution Effect and the Failure of the Status Quo}
The Status Quo approach, heavily reliant on Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) campaigns distributed equally across the municipality, ends with a terminal compliance of 30.18\% (Table~\ref{tab:policy_comparison}). This trajectory computationally illustrates within the model the limitations of the ``Value-Action Gap''---also referred to as the intention-action gap in environmental psychology \citep{Geiger2021, Liao2024}. While IEC campaigns may marginally increase the environmental attitude ($w_a$) of the household agents, passive education fails to build habit formation when the physical friction of segregation remains unaddressed \citep{Moeini2023, Yazawa2025}. By spreading limited funds equally, the Local Government Unit (LGU) dilutes its capacity, ensuring that the critical density of policing or the minimum per-capita incentive threshold is never met in any single barangay \citep{Alphonso2024}. 

\subsection{The Plateau of Single-Instrument Regimes}
Similarly, the simulation revealed the inherent behavioral ceilings of single-instrument regimes. The Pure Enforcement strategy collapses completely by Quarter 2 (as shown in Table~\ref{tab:policy_comparison}), while the Pure Incentives strategy stagnates at a terminal compliance of only 15.91\% by Year 3. 

In the Pure Enforcement scenario, the uniform distribution of penal resources meant that dense urban centers like Barangay Poblacion simply overwhelmed the available enforcers, a phenomenon consistent with the \textit{Urban Resource Trap} \citep{Villanueva2021}. The probability of a non-compliant household being caught remained statistically insignificant, neutralizing the deterrent effect of the policy \citep{Badua2022}. Conversely, the Pure Incentives scenario failed due to the diminishing per-capita value of rewards \citep{MuZhang2021}. When a fixed municipal budget is divided equally among a large population, the individual payout drops below the household's ``Cost of Effort'' ($c_{\text{effort}}$) threshold, rendering the financial motivation insufficient to overcome the logistical inconvenience of waste sorting \citep{Chen2023, Corcoran2020}.

\subsection{Sequential Saturation: The HuDRL Advantage}
In stark contrast, the HuDRL Agent successfully navigated these socio-economic trade-offs by dynamically shifting resources, achieving an exceptional terminal Global Compliance Rate of 90.45\% by Quarter 12 \citep{Jimenez2025, ZhengAI2022}. This represents a massive deviation from the stagnant, linear trajectories of the static models. 

Rather than suffering from a slow exploration phase, the adaptive strategy broke the municipality's inertia almost immediately, surging to 73.65\% compliance by the end of Year 1. This overwhelming success is attributed directly to the agent's discovery of the ``Sequential Saturation'' logic. The AI recognized that overcoming the urban density barrier required abandoning the heuristic of simultaneous, equal distribution. Instead, the agent utilized sequential dominance---concentrating its budget to artificially induce a behavioral tipping point in specific barangays \citep{Centola2018, Nyborg2024}, locking in compliance through social norms, and subsequently reallocating those funds to besiege the next resistant population center \citep{Nishimura2022}. This dynamic phasing indicates that in resource-constrained LGUs, the timing and concentration of capital are far more critical than the absolute budget size \citep{Jimenez2025}.
