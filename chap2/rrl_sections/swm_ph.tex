
To accurately simulate the complex interplay between municipal policy and household behavior, computational tools must reflect the decentralized realities of the governance structure. This section reviews the methodological shift towards Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) and justifies the hierarchical, spatially constrained architecture utilized in this study.

\subsection{Moving Beyond Static Optimization}

Historically, the optimization of Solid Waste Management (SWM) systems has relied heavily on traditional equation-based mathematical models, such as Linear Programming (LP) or System Dynamics (SD) \citep{Zhang2021, Saif2022}. While highly effective for solving macro-logistical problems like vehicle routing or facility location \citep{Akbarpour2021}, these top-down models fundamentally assume homogeneous populations acting with perfect rationality. They fail to capture the non-linear, adaptive nature of human behavior, often treating public compliance as a static, deterministic input rather than a dynamic variable \citep{TianReview2024}.

Agent-Based Models (ABMs) address this limitation by utilizing a bottom-up computational approach. In an ABM, a system is modeled as a collection of autonomous decision-making entities (agents), each endowed with unique socio-economic attributes, psychological states, and behavioral heuristics \citep{Meng2018}. By simulating the micro-level interactions between these heterogeneous agents and their environment, ABMs allow researchers to observe the ``emergence'' of macro-level phenomena—such as the rapid cascade of social norms or the sudden collapse of compliance due to policy fatigue \citep{Ma2023, deSouza2021}. Consequently, ABM has been recognized as a superior methodological framework for testing behavioral SWM policies prior to real-world implementation \citep{TianReview2024}.

\subsection{Hierarchical and Multi-Agent Architectures}

While ABM is increasingly utilized in environmental modeling, the majority of existing SWM simulations employ a ``flat'' architecture, placing all household agents within a single, uniform municipal environment \citep{Ding2024}. However, as established in Section 2.1, the empirical reality of Philippine governance is strictly decentralized and multi-level. 

To bridge this gap, recent literature advocates for multi-level or hierarchical ABMs that accurately reflect polycentric governance structures \citep{Brugiere2022}. This study operationalizes this by introducing a nested multi-agent architecture. Specifically, \texttt{HouseholdAgents} are not governed directly by the municipality; rather, they are nested within distinct \texttt{BarangayAgents}. These \texttt{BarangayAgents} act as localized environments with their own independent baseline budgets and demographic profiles \citep{TianCase2024}. 

Above this operates the central municipal \textit{Mayor Agent}, representing a macro-level executive that dynamically allocates augmentation funds across the barangays \citep{ZhengAI2022}. This hierarchical design is theoretically crucial, as it allows the simulation to capture the friction of policy translation—where municipal mandates are filtered, and sometimes bottlenecked, by the fiscal and administrative realities of the frontline barangay implementers \citep{Yazawa2025}.

\subsection{Bounded Rationality and Spatial Enforcement}

A critical component of policy simulation is the realistic modeling of environmental policing. Traditional models frequently assume omniscient enforcement, where any violation within the municipality is instantly detected and penalized. This contradicts the empirical realities of underfunded Local Government Units (LGUs), where enforcement is sporadic and highly localized \citep{Dalugdog2021}.

This study aligns its enforcement mechanics with the principles of bounded rationality and ``green criminology.'' \citet{Carpio2025} notes that household waste violations are often perceived as minor, victimless offenses, leading to widespread normalization unless physical enforcement is visible. Because LGUs cannot afford ubiquitous monitoring, the deterrence effect of the ``No Segregation, No Collection'' policy is strictly constrained by spatial proximity \citep{Badua2022}. 

To model this accurately, the simulation deploys \texttt{EnforcementAgents} operating under bounded spatial awareness. Rather than possessing municipality-wide detection capabilities, these agents execute localized, line-of-sight patrols \citep{Meng2018}. A household's Perceived Probability of Detection is therefore not a static municipal constant, but a dynamic, spatially dependent variable that spikes only when an \texttt{EnforcementAgent} physically patrols their immediate neighborhood \citep{Jimenez2025}. This spatial limitation accurately forces the AI (the Mayor Agent) to grapple with the real-world logistical challenge of policing a large municipality with a severely limited workforce, providing a highly realistic testbed for optimizing targeted enforcement strategies.
